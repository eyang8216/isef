D2: Electric and Magnetic Fields

Syllabus Code: D2 | Level: HL

Learning Objectives

  • Define and calculate electric field strength as force per unit charge and understand the relationship E = F/q
  • Describe and sketch electric field patterns for point charges, parallel plates, and charged spheres
  • Calculate electric potential difference and understand the relationship between field strength and potential gradient
  • Apply the equations for work done in moving charges through electric fields
  • Analyze motion of charged particles in uniform electric fields using kinematic and dynamic principles
  • Define magnetic flux density and calculate the force on a moving charge in a magnetic field using F = qvB
  • Determine the force on a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field using F = BIL
  • Analyze the motion of charged particles in uniform magnetic fields including circular motion and velocity selection
  • Explain electromagnetic induction using Faraday's law and calculate induced EMF from changing magnetic flux
  • Apply Lenz's law to determine the direction of induced currents and explain energy conservation
  • Derive and apply equations for motional EMF in conductors moving through magnetic fields
  • Analyze transformer operation including ideal and non-ideal cases (HL)
  • Solve problems involving combined electric and magnetic fields including velocity selectors and mass spectrometers (HL)
  • Understand and apply the Hall effect to determine charge carrier properties (HL)

Key Definitions

Electric Field Strength (E)
The force per unit positive charge experienced by a small test charge placed in the field. Measured in N C⁻¹ or V m⁻¹. Vector quantity pointing in the direction a positive charge would move.
Electric Potential (V)
The work done per unit charge in bringing a small positive test charge from infinity to that point in the field. Measured in volts (V). Scalar quantity.
Equipotential Surface
A surface where all points have the same electric potential. No work is done moving a charge along an equipotential surface. Always perpendicular to field lines.
Magnetic Flux Density (B)
The force per unit current per unit length on a conductor perpendicular to the field. Measured in tesla (T) or Wb m⁻². Vector quantity indicating field direction.
Magnetic Flux (Φ)
The product of magnetic flux density and the perpendicular area through which it passes: Φ = BA cos θ. Measured in webers (Wb). Represents the 'amount' of magnetic field passing through a surface.
Electromagnetic Induction
The production of an electromotive force (EMF) across a conductor when exposed to a changing magnetic flux. Basis of generators, transformers, and many electrical devices.
Faraday's Law
The magnitude of induced EMF equals the rate of change of magnetic flux linkage: ε = -N(dΦ/dt), where N is the number of turns.
Lenz's Law
The direction of induced current is such that it opposes the change in magnetic flux that produced it. Consequence of energy conservation.
Motional EMF
The EMF induced in a conductor moving through a magnetic field, given by ε = BLv for a straight conductor of length L moving perpendicular to field B with velocity v.
Hall Effect (HL)
The production of a voltage across a conductor perpendicular to both current flow and magnetic field. Used to determine charge carrier type and density.

Introduction to Electric and Magnetic Fields

Electric and magnetic fields represent two fundamental aspects of electromagnetic force, one of the four fundamental forces of nature. While historically treated separately, Maxwell's equations unified them into electromagnetism, showing they are two aspects of the same phenomenon. This topic builds on your understanding of forces and energy from mechanics, applying similar principles to charged particles.

Why This Matters: Understanding electric and magnetic fields is essential for:

  • Modern technology (motors, generators, transformers, particle accelerators)
  • Medical imaging (MRI scanners use strong magnetic fields)
  • Energy generation and transmission
  • Fundamental physics research (particle physics, plasma physics)

Prerequisites: Before starting D2, you should be comfortable with:

  • Vector addition and resolution (Topic 1)
  • Newton's laws and circular motion (Topics 2.1-2.3)
  • Work, energy, and power (Topic 2.4)
  • Electric charge and current (Topic 5)

The mathematical demand increases at HL level with more complex problem-solving involving combined fields and calculus-based concepts.

D2.1: Electric Fields - Fundamentals

Field Concept: A field is a region of space where a force is experienced. Electric fields exist around any charged object and exert forces on other charges within the field.

Electric Field Strength

Electric field strength \(E\) at a point is defined as the force per unit positive charge:

$$E = \frac{F}{q}$$

where \(F\) is the force (N) and \(q\) is the test charge (C). Units: N C⁻¹ or V m⁻¹ (these are equivalent).

Rearranging: \(F = qE\)

This tells us the force experienced by any charge \(q\) placed in field \(E\). If \(q\) is negative, the force is opposite to the field direction.

Key Insight: Electric field is a property of the space around charges, independent of any test charge. We define it using a test charge, but once defined, the field exists whether or not a charge is present to experience it.

Uniform Electric Fields

Between two parallel charged plates, the field is approximately uniform (constant magnitude and direction) except near the edges. For plates separated by distance \(d\) with potential difference \(V\):

$$E = \frac{V}{d}$$

This shows the alternative unit: V m⁻¹. The field points from positive to negative plate.

Radial Electric Fields

For a point charge \(Q\) (or spherically symmetric charge distribution), the field at distance \(r\) is:

$$E = \frac{kQ}{r^2}$$

where \(k = 8.99 \times 10^9\) N m² C⁻² (or \(k = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\) where \(\epsilon_0\) is permittivity of free space).

Field lines radiate outward from positive charges, inward toward negative charges. The field strength decreases with the inverse square of distance - double the distance, quarter the field strength.

Common Misconception: Students often confuse the field created BY a charge with the force ON a charge. \(E = kQ/r^2\) gives the field created by charge \(Q\). To find force on another charge \(q\) at that location, use \(F = qE\).

D2.1: Electric Potential and Potential Energy

Electric Potential

Electric potential \(V\) at a point is the work done per unit charge in bringing a small positive test charge from infinity to that point:

$$V = \frac{W}{q}$$

Units: joules per coulomb = volts (V). Potential is a scalar quantity (no direction).

For a point charge \(Q\):

$$V = \frac{kQ}{r}$$

Note: This decreases as \(1/r\), not \(1/r^2\). Potential falls off more slowly than field strength.

Relationship Between Field and Potential

In a uniform field:

$$E = -\frac{\Delta V}{\Delta d}$$

The negative sign indicates field points from high to low potential (downhill). Think of potential like gravitational height - objects naturally fall to lower potential.

Work and Energy in Electric Fields

Work done moving charge \(q\) through potential difference \(\Delta V\):

$$W = q\Delta V$$

For a charge moving in a uniform field over distance \(d\) parallel to the field:

$$W = Fd = qEd = q\Delta V$$

Energy Conservation: For a particle accelerated from rest through potential difference \(V\):

$$qV = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$$

This fundamental equation links field quantities to motion - essential for many IB problems.

Equipotential Surfaces

Equipotential surfaces connect points of equal potential. Key properties:

  • No work is done moving along an equipotential (since \(W = q\Delta V\) and \(\Delta V = 0\))
  • Always perpendicular to field lines
  • For parallel plates: flat planes parallel to the plates
  • For point charges: spherical surfaces centered on the charge
  • Closer spacing indicates stronger field (steeper potential gradient)

D2.1: Motion of Charged Particles in Electric Fields

Acceleration in Uniform Fields

When a charged particle enters a uniform electric field, it experiences constant force \(F = qE\), giving constant acceleration:

$$a = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{qE}{m}$$

This is analogous to projectile motion in a gravitational field:

  • If the particle enters perpendicular to the field, it follows a parabolic path
  • If parallel to the field, it undergoes uniform acceleration (like free fall)

Method for solving problems:

  1. Identify initial conditions (velocity, position, field strength)
  2. Calculate force: \(F = qE\)
  3. Calculate acceleration: \(a = F/m\)
  4. Apply kinematic equations (SUVAT)
  5. Consider components separately for 2D motion

D2.2: Magnetic Fields - Fundamentals

Magnetic Flux Density

Magnetic flux density \(B\) (often called magnetic field strength) is defined through the force on a current-carrying conductor:

$$F = BIL\sin\theta$$

where \(I\) is current (A), \(L\) is length of conductor in field (m), and \(\theta\) is angle between field and current direction.

Units: tesla (T) = N A⁻¹ m⁻¹ = Wb m⁻²

For perpendicular orientation (\(\theta = 90°\)): \(F = BIL\)

Force on a Moving Charge

Since current is moving charge, we can express force on a single charge:

$$F = qvB\sin\theta$$

For perpendicular motion (\(\theta = 90°\)):

$$F = qvB$$

Direction: Use Fleming's left-hand rule (motor rule):

  • First finger: Field direction (N to S)
  • Second finger: Current direction (conventional, positive to negative)
  • Thumb: Force direction
Common Misconception: Magnetic force does no work. Why? Force is always perpendicular to velocity (\(F \perp v\)), so \(W = Fd\cos 90° = 0\). Magnetic fields change direction of motion but not speed.

D2.2: Motion of Charged Particles in Magnetic Fields

Circular Motion in Uniform Fields

When a charged particle enters a uniform magnetic field perpendicular to its velocity, it follows a circular path. The magnetic force provides centripetal force:

$$qvB = \frac{mv^2}{r}$$

Solving for radius:

$$r = \frac{mv}{qB}$$

This is the radius of curvature - larger for faster or heavier particles, smaller for stronger fields or higher charge.

Period of circular motion:

$$T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = \frac{2\pi m}{qB}$$

Key Insight: Period is independent of velocity. Faster particles travel in larger circles but complete them in the same time.

Velocity Selector (HL)

Combining perpendicular electric and magnetic fields creates a velocity selector - only particles with specific velocity pass straight through.

For a particle to travel undeflected:

$$qE = qvB$$

$$v = \frac{E}{B}$$

Hall Effect (HL)

When current flows through a conductor in a magnetic field, charge carriers experience a sideways force, creating a voltage across the conductor perpendicular to both current and field.

Hall Voltage:

$$V_H = \frac{BI}{ntq}$$

where \(n\) is charge carrier density, \(t\) is conductor thickness.

D2.3: Electromagnetic Induction - Faraday's Law

Magnetic Flux

Magnetic flux \(\Phi\) through a surface measures the 'amount' of magnetic field passing through:

$$\Phi = BA\cos\theta$$

where \(A\) is area (m²), \(\theta\) is angle between field and normal to surface. Units: weber (Wb) = T m²

Faraday's Law of Electromagnetic Induction

The magnitude of induced EMF equals the rate of change of flux linkage:

$$\varepsilon = -N\frac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t}$$

The negative sign (from Lenz's law) indicates opposition to the change.

Three Ways to Change Flux:

  1. Change \(B\) (varying magnetic field strength)
  2. Change \(A\) (varying area in field)
  3. Change \(\theta\) (rotating coil in field)

Lenz's Law

The direction of induced current opposes the change in flux that caused it.

Method for applying Lenz's law:

  1. Identify the flux change (increasing/decreasing, which direction)
  2. Determine what field the induced current must create to oppose this change
  3. Use right-hand grip rule to find current direction for that field

D2.3: Motional EMF and Applications

Motional EMF

When a straight conductor of length \(L\) moves perpendicular to a magnetic field \(B\) with velocity \(v\), an EMF is induced:

$$\varepsilon = BLv$$

AC Generator Principle

A rotating coil in a magnetic field generates alternating EMF:

$$\varepsilon = NAB\omega\sin(\omega t)$$

Peak EMF: \(\varepsilon_0 = NAB\omega\)

Transformers (with HL Extensions)

Ideal Transformer Equations:

$$\frac{V_s}{V_p} = \frac{N_s}{N_p}$$

$$\frac{I_s}{I_p} = \frac{N_p}{N_s}$$

where subscripts \(p\) = primary, \(s\) = secondary.

Power Conservation: \(P_{in} = P_{out}\) for ideal transformer:

$$V_pI_p = V_sI_s$$

Real Transformers (HL):

Efficiency:

$$\eta = \frac{P_{out}}{P_{in}} \times 100\%$$

Energy losses include resistance losses, eddy current losses, hysteresis losses, and flux leakage. Typical efficiency: 95-99%.

Practice Problems

Problem 1
A uniform electric field of magnitude 450 N C⁻¹ exists between two parallel plates separated by 3.0 cm. (a) Calculate the potential difference between the plates. (b) An electron is released from rest at the negative plate. Calculate its speed when it reaches the positive plate. (electron mass = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg, charge = -1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C)
Solution:
(a) V = Ed = 450 × 0.030 = 13.5 V

(b) Using energy conservation: qV = ½mv²
v = √(2qV/m) = √(2 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 13.5 / 9.11 × 10⁻³¹)
v = √(4.74 × 10¹²) = 2.18 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹

Explanation: Part (a) uses the uniform field relationship E = V/d. Part (b) applies energy conservation - the electric potential energy is converted to kinetic energy. Note we use the magnitude of charge in the energy equation since we're calculating speed (magnitude of velocity).
Problem 2
Two point charges, +4.0 μC and -2.0 μC, are separated by 0.60 m in a vacuum. (a) Calculate the electric field strength at the midpoint between them. (b) Calculate the electric potential at the midpoint. (k = 8.99 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻²)
Solution:
(a) At midpoint, distance from each charge: r = 0.30 m
Field from +4.0 μC (pointing away): E₁ = kQ₁/r² = (8.99 × 10⁹ × 4.0 × 10⁻⁶)/(0.30)² = 4.0 × 10⁵ N C⁻¹ (to the right)
Field from -2.0 μC (pointing toward): E₂ = kQ₂/r² = (8.99 × 10⁹ × 2.0 × 10⁻⁶)/(0.30)² = 2.0 × 10⁵ N C⁻¹ (to the right)
Total field: E = E₁ + E₂ = 6.0 × 10⁵ N C⁻¹ (to the right, toward negative charge)

(b) Potential is scalar, add algebraically:
V = kQ₁/r + kQ₂/r = (8.99 × 10⁹/0.30) × (4.0 × 10⁻⁶ - 2.0 × 10⁻⁶)
V = 3.0 × 10¹⁰ × 2.0 × 10⁻⁶ = 6.0 × 10⁴ V

Explanation: This tests understanding of field (vector) vs potential (scalar). For the field, both contributions point in the same direction, so they add. For potential, we add algebraically considering signs.
Problem 3
A proton (mass 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, charge +1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) enters a uniform magnetic field of 0.50 T with velocity 2.0 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹ perpendicular to the field. (a) Calculate the radius of the circular path. (b) Calculate the period of revolution. (c) Explain why the kinetic energy of the proton remains constant.
Solution:
(a) Using qvB = mv²/r:
r = mv/(qB) = (1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ × 2.0 × 10⁶)/(1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 0.50)
r = 3.34 × 10⁻²¹/8.0 × 10⁻²⁰ = 0.042 m = 4.2 cm

(b) T = 2πm/(qB) = (2π × 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷)/(1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 0.50)
T = 1.05 × 10⁻²⁶/8.0 × 10⁻²⁰ = 1.31 × 10⁻⁷ s = 131 ns

(c) The magnetic force is always perpendicular to the velocity (F ⊥ v). Since work = Fd cos θ and θ = 90°, no work is done by the magnetic force. Therefore, kinetic energy (½mv²) remains constant. The force changes direction but not speed.

Explanation: This is a fundamental application of circular motion in magnetic fields. Note that the period is independent of velocity - this is the cyclotron principle.
Problem 4
A rectangular coil of dimensions 5.0 cm × 8.0 cm with 200 turns is placed in a uniform magnetic field of 0.40 T. The coil is initially perpendicular to the field. (a) Calculate the initial magnetic flux through the coil. (b) The coil is rotated through 90° in 0.20 s. Calculate the average induced EMF. (c) State the direction of the induced current when the coil is rotated so the flux decreases.
Solution:
(a) Area A = 0.050 × 0.080 = 4.0 × 10⁻³ m²
Initial flux (perpendicular): Φᵢ = BA = 0.40 × 4.0 × 10⁻³ = 1.6 × 10⁻³ Wb

(b) After 90° rotation, coil is parallel to field: Φf = 0
Change in flux linkage: NΔΦ = 200 × (0 - 1.6 × 10⁻³) = -0.32 Wb
Average induced EMF: ε = -NΔΦ/Δt = -(-0.32)/0.20 = 1.6 V

(c) By Lenz's law, the induced current creates a magnetic field to oppose the decrease in flux. Therefore, the induced current produces a field in the same direction as the original field through the coil.

Explanation: Part (a) tests flux calculation with perpendicular orientation. Part (b) uses Faraday's law - note the final flux is zero when parallel to the field. Part (c) requires application of Lenz's law conceptually.
Problem 5
A straight wire of length 0.25 m moves perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field of 0.80 T at a constant velocity of 4.0 m s⁻¹. (a) Calculate the induced EMF across the ends of the wire. (b) If the wire forms part of a closed circuit with total resistance 2.0 Ω, calculate the current induced. (c) Calculate the magnetic force on the wire due to this current. (d) Calculate the mechanical power required to maintain constant velocity.
Solution:
(a) ε = BLv = 0.80 × 0.25 × 4.0 = 0.80 V

(b) I = ε/R = 0.80/2.0 = 0.40 A

(c) F = BIL = 0.80 × 0.40 × 0.25 = 0.080 N
Direction: By Lenz's law, this force opposes the motion.

(d) P = Fv = 0.080 × 4.0 = 0.32 W
Alternatively: P = ε²/R = (0.80)²/2.0 = 0.32 W ✓

Explanation: This demonstrates energy conservation in electromagnetic induction. The mechanical power input equals the electrical power dissipated (I²R = 0.32 W). The magnetic force opposes motion - you must do work against it to maintain constant velocity.
Problem 6
An ideal transformer is used to step down voltage from 240 V to 12 V to power a device that draws 3.0 A at 12 V. (a) Calculate the turns ratio Nₚ/Nₛ. (b) Calculate the current in the primary coil. (c) A real transformer with 92% efficiency delivers the same output. Calculate the input power required.
Solution:
(a) Nₚ/Nₛ = Vₚ/Vₛ = 240/12 = 20

(b) For ideal transformer: VₚIₚ = VₛIₛ
Iₚ = (VₛIₛ)/Vₚ = (12 × 3.0)/240 = 0.15 A

(c) Output power: Pₒᵤₜ = VₛIₛ = 12 × 3.0 = 36 W
Efficiency: η = Pₒᵤₜ/Pᵢₙ
Pᵢₙ = Pₒᵤₜ/η = 36/0.92 = 39.1 W

Explanation: This tests understanding of ideal vs real transformers. Note that for the ideal transformer, power is conserved exactly. For the real transformer, input power must be higher to account for losses.
Problem 7 (HL)
A velocity selector uses perpendicular electric and magnetic fields. The electric field strength is 2.5 × 10⁴ V m⁻¹ and the magnetic flux density is 0.080 T. (a) Calculate the velocity of particles that pass straight through. (b) A proton (q = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, m = 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg) with this velocity then enters a region with magnetic field only (0.080 T). Calculate the radius of its circular path.
Solution:
(a) For undeflected motion: qE = qvB
v = E/B = (2.5 × 10⁴)/(0.080) = 3.125 × 10⁵ m s⁻¹

(b) r = mv/(qB) = (1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ × 3.125 × 10⁵)/(1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 0.080)
r = 5.22 × 10⁻²²/(1.28 × 10⁻²⁰) = 0.041 m = 4.1 cm

Explanation: This is a typical HL problem combining velocity selection with mass spectrometry principles. Part (a) shows the selected velocity is independent of particle charge or mass - only field strengths matter.
Problem 8 (HL)
A charged particle is accelerated from rest through a potential difference of 5000 V. It then enters a region where electric and magnetic fields are perpendicular to each other and to the particle's velocity. The electric field strength is 8.0 × 10³ N C⁻¹ and the magnetic flux density is 0.040 T. The particle travels undeflected. Calculate the charge-to-mass ratio (q/m) of the particle.
Solution:
Step 1: Find velocity from acceleration
qV = ½mv²
v² = 2qV/m
v = √(2qV/m)

Step 2: Apply velocity selector condition
For undeflected: qE = qvB
v = E/B = (8.0 × 10³)/(0.040) = 2.0 × 10⁵ m s⁻¹

Step 3: Combine equations
E/B = √(2qV/m)
(E/B)² = 2qV/m
q/m = (E/B)²/(2V) = (2.0 × 10⁵)²/(2 × 5000)
q/m = 4.0 × 10¹⁰/(1.0 × 10⁴) = 4.0 × 10⁶ C kg⁻¹

Explanation: This multi-step HL problem requires combining energy conservation with the velocity selector principle. The key insight is that the accelerating voltage determines velocity based on q/m ratio.

Exam Tips

Always draw a diagram for field problems - it helps visualize field directions, forces, and particle trajectories. Include clear labels for all vectors.
When using F = qvB sin θ or similar equations, check whether the angle is 90° (perpendicular) - many exam questions involve perpendicular orientations where sin θ = 1.
Remember that magnetic forces do NO work - this is a common conceptual question. The force is always perpendicular to velocity, so W = Fd cos 90° = 0.
For Lenz's law problems, use a systematic method: (1) identify flux change direction, (2) determine opposing field needed, (3) apply right-hand rule for current direction. Don't skip steps.
In transformer problems, watch the ratio carefully: voltage ratio = turns ratio, but current ratio is INVERTED. Many students mix these up.
For combined field problems (HL), set up force balance equations carefully. If a particle is undeflected, all forces sum to zero: F_E + F_B = 0 (vector sum).
Energy conservation is powerful for electric field problems - often easier than using forces and kinematics. Use qΔV = ΔKE for particles accelerated through potential differences.
When calculating flux Φ = BA cos θ, remember θ is the angle between field B and the normal to the area, NOT between B and the plane itself.
For circular motion in magnetic fields, remember that period T = 2πm/(qB) is independent of velocity - this seems counterintuitive but is crucial for cyclotron operation.
Check that your answers have sensible magnitudes. If you calculate an electron's radius as 3 meters or a magnetic field as 10^15 tesla, you've made an error.
Units are often worth marks - write them clearly. For B, use tesla (T). For Φ, use weber (Wb). For E fields, use V m⁻¹ or N C⁻¹ (both are correct).
In data-based questions, always reference the data or graph explicitly: "From the graph, at t = 2.0 s, the velocity is 3.5 m s⁻¹..." Show you're using the data provided.
For 'explain' questions about induction, mentioning both Faraday's law (changing flux induces EMF) AND Lenz's law (direction opposes change) typically earns full marks.
Hall effect problems (HL) require careful identification of directions - sketch the forces on charge carriers to determine which side becomes positive/negative.
In mass spectrometer problems (HL), remember that only the m/q ratio is directly measured - you need additional information to separate mass from charge effects.

Problem-Solving Strategies

General Approach for Field Problems

  1. Identify the field type and configuration - Uniform or radial? Electric, magnetic, or both? What creates the field?
  2. Choose the appropriate equations - For forces: F = qE or F = qvB sin θ; For energy: W = qΔV or qV = ½mv²; For circular motion: qvB = mv²/r
  3. Consider directions carefully - Electric field: direction of force on positive charge; Magnetic force: use Fleming's left-hand rule; Induced current: apply Lenz's law systematically
  4. Check units - Convert to SI units before calculating; Check final answer has sensible units and magnitude

Common Pitfalls

  • Confusing field and force: Field exists independently; force depends on charge present
  • Sign errors with charges: Negative charges experience force opposite to field direction
  • Angle in magnetic force: F = qvB sin θ - perpendicular gives maximum force
  • Work done by magnetic fields: Always zero - force perpendicular to displacement
  • Direction of induced current: Don't just guess - apply Lenz's law systematically
  • Transformer equations: Nₚ/Nₛ ratio appears differently in voltage and current equations
