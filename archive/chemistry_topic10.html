Key Definitions

Homologous series
A family of organic compounds with the same functional group and general formula, where successive members differ by CH2. Members show a gradual change in physical properties and similar chemical properties.
Functional group
An atom or group of atoms responsible for the characteristic chemical reactions of a compound. Examples include hydroxyl (-OH), carbonyl (C=O), carboxyl (-COOH), and amino (-NH2).
Structural isomers
Compounds with the same molecular formula but different structural arrangements of atoms. Types include chain isomers, position isomers, and functional group isomers.
Stereoisomers
Compounds with the same structural formula but different spatial arrangements of atoms. Include geometric (cis-trans, E-Z) and optical (enantiomers) isomers.
Chiral center
A carbon atom bonded to four different groups, resulting in non-superimposable mirror images (enantiomers). Also called an asymmetric carbon or stereocenter.
Enantiomers
Non-superimposable mirror image stereoisomers that rotate plane-polarized light in opposite directions. Have identical physical properties except optical activity.
Nucleophile
An electron-rich species with a lone pair or negative charge that is attracted to electron-deficient centers. Examples include OH-, CN-, NH3, and H2O.
Electrophile
An electron-deficient species that accepts electron pairs. Examples include H+, Br+, NO2+, and carbocations.
Free radical
A highly reactive species with an unpaired electron, formed by homolytic fission. Involved in chain reactions such as halogenation of alkanes.
Markovnikov's rule
In addition reactions to unsymmetrical alkenes, the hydrogen atom adds to the carbon with more hydrogen atoms already attached, and the other group adds to the carbon with fewer hydrogens.
Retrosynthetic analysis
A problem-solving technique for planning organic synthesis by working backwards from the target molecule to identify suitable starting materials and reaction sequences.

10.1 Fundamentals of Organic Chemistry

Organic chemistry is the study of carbon-containing compounds. Carbon's unique ability to form four covalent bonds and catenate (form chains) creates millions of possible organic molecules.

Structural Formulas

Three main ways to represent organic molecules:

  1. Full structural formula: Shows all atoms and bonds explicitly. For ethanol: H-C-C-O-H with all H atoms shown.
  2. Condensed structural formula: Groups atoms together for efficiency. Ethanol: CH3CH2OH or C2H5OH.
  3. Skeletal formula: Shows only carbon skeleton and functional groups. Each vertex/end represents a carbon atom; hydrogen atoms on carbon are implied. This is the most efficient representation and preferred in IB exams.

IUPAC Nomenclature System

Systematic naming follows these steps:

  1. Identify the longest carbon chain (parent chain) - this determines the root name (meth-, eth-, prop-, but-, pent-, hex-)
  2. Identify the functional group - this determines the suffix and numbering direction
  3. Number the chain from the end giving functional groups the lowest numbers
  4. Name and number substituents (branches) - use prefixes di-, tri-, tetra- for multiple identical groups
  5. Assemble the name: substituents alphabetically + parent chain + functional group suffix

Priority order of functional groups (highest to lowest):
Carboxylic acid (-COOH) > Aldehyde (-CHO) > Ketone (C=O) > Alcohol (-OH) > Alkene (C=C) > Alkane (C-C)

Example: CH3CH(CH3)CH2CH2OH

  • Longest chain: 4 carbons (butanol)
  • Functional group: alcohol (-OH) on carbon 1
  • Substituent: methyl on carbon 3
  • Name: 3-methylbutan-1-ol

Common Functional Groups

  • Alkanes (C-C): suffix -ane, saturated hydrocarbons
  • Alkenes (C=C): suffix -ene, unsaturated with double bonds
  • Alcohols (-OH): suffix -ol, hydroxyl group
  • Aldehydes (-CHO): suffix -al, carbonyl at chain end
  • Ketones (C=O): suffix -one, carbonyl within chain
  • Carboxylic acids (-COOH): suffix -oic acid, carboxyl group
  • Halogenoalkanes (C-X): prefix halo- (chloro-, bromo-, iodo-)
  • Amines (-NH2): suffix -amine or prefix amino-

Why this matters: Functional groups determine reactivity. The same functional group in different molecules undergoes similar reactions, allowing prediction of chemical behavior.

10.2 Functional Group Chemistry

10.2.1 Alkanes

Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons with only single C-C and C-H bonds. General formula: \(C_nH_{2n+2}\)

Reactions of Alkanes:

1. Complete combustion (excess oxygen):

$$C_nH_{2n+2} + \frac{3n+1}{2}O_2 \rightarrow nCO_2 + (n+1)H_2O$$

Example: \(C_3H_8 + 5O_2 \rightarrow 3CO_2 + 4H_2O\) (exothermic, \(\Delta H\) negative)

2. Incomplete combustion (limited oxygen):
Produces CO and/or C (soot): \(2C_3H_8 + 7O_2 \rightarrow 6CO + 8H_2O\)

3. Free radical substitution with halogens (UV light required):

Initiation: \(Cl_2 \xrightarrow{UV} 2Cl\cdot\) (homolytic fission)

Propagation:
\(CH_4 + Cl\cdot \rightarrow \cdot CH_3 + HCl\)
\(\cdot CH_3 + Cl_2 \rightarrow CH_3Cl + Cl\cdot\)

Termination: \(Cl\cdot + Cl\cdot \rightarrow Cl_2\) (or other radical combinations)

This mechanism explains why alkanes are relatively unreactive - the C-H and C-C bonds are strong and non-polar.

10.2.2 Alkenes

Alkenes contain C=C double bonds. General formula: \(C_nH_{2n}\)

The double bond consists of one sigma bond and one pi bond. The pi bond is weaker and more reactive, making alkenes much more reactive than alkanes.

Reactions of Alkenes:

1. Hydrogenation (addition of H2):

$$C_2H_4 + H_2 \xrightarrow{Ni, 150°C} C_2H_6$$

Converts alkenes to alkanes; used in margarine production.

2. Halogenation (addition of Br2 or Cl2):

$$C_2H_4 + Br_2 \rightarrow CH_2BrCH_2Br$$

This is a test for unsaturation: bromine water (orange-brown) decolorizes when alkene is present.

3. Hydrohalogenation (addition of HX):

$$C_2H_4 + HBr \rightarrow CH_3CH_2Br$$

For unsymmetrical alkenes, follows Markovnikov's rule:

$$CH_3CH=CH_2 + HBr \rightarrow CH_3CHBrCH_3$$ (major product, not \(CH_3CH_2CH_2Br\))

4. Hydration (addition of H2O):

$$C_2H_4 + H_2O \xrightarrow{H_3PO_4, 300°C, 60 atm} CH_3CH_2OH$$

Produces alcohols; industrially important.

5. Addition polymerization:

$$nCH_2=CH_2 \rightarrow (-CH_2-CH_2-)_n$$

Produces polymers like polyethylene, polypropylene, PVC.

10.2.3 Distinguishing Alkanes and Alkenes

Test: Add bromine water

  • Alkene: Orange-brown color disappears (addition reaction)
  • Alkane: No change (no reaction without UV light)

10.2.4 Alcohols

Alcohols contain the hydroxyl functional group (-OH). Classification:

  • Primary (1°): -OH on carbon bonded to one other carbon
  • Secondary (2°): -OH on carbon bonded to two other carbons
  • Tertiary (3°): -OH on carbon bonded to three other carbons

Reactions of Alcohols:

1. Combustion:

$$C_2H_5OH + 3O_2 \rightarrow 2CO_2 + 3H_2O$$

Basis for use as fuel (ethanol in gasoline).

2. Oxidation (using acidified dichromate, \(K_2Cr_2O_7/H^+\)):

Primary alcohols:

$$RCH_2OH \xrightarrow{[O]} RCHO \xrightarrow{[O]} RCOOH$$

Alcohol → Aldehyde → Carboxylic acid

Example: \(CH_3CH_2OH \rightarrow CH_3CHO \rightarrow CH_3COOH\)

To isolate aldehyde: distill immediately (aldehydes have lower boiling points)
To produce carboxylic acid: use excess oxidizing agent and reflux

Secondary alcohols:

$$R_2CHOH \xrightarrow{[O]} R_2CO$$

Alcohol → Ketone (cannot be further oxidized)

Tertiary alcohols: Resistant to oxidation (no H on C-OH)

Color change: Orange dichromate → Green \(Cr^{3+}\) (oxidation occurred)

3. Substitution to form halogenoalkanes:

$$CH_3CH_2OH + HBr \rightarrow CH_3CH_2Br + H_2O$$

Or using \(PCl_5\): \(CH_3CH_2OH + PCl_5 \rightarrow CH_3CH_2Cl + POCl_3 + HCl\)

10.2.5 Halogenoalkanes

Contain C-X bonds (X = F, Cl, Br, I). The C-X bond is polar (\(C^{\delta+}-X^{\delta-}\)) making the carbon susceptible to nucleophilic attack.

Nucleophilic substitution reactions:

1. Hydrolysis (with aqueous OH-):

$$CH_3CH_2Br + OH^- \rightarrow CH_3CH_2OH + Br^-$$

Halogenoalkane → Alcohol

2. Reaction with cyanide (CN-):

$$CH_3Br + CN^- \rightarrow CH_3CN + Br^-$$

Increases carbon chain length by one.

3. Reaction with ammonia (NH3):

$$CH_3Br + 2NH_3 \rightarrow CH_3NH_2 + NH_4Br$$

Produces primary amines.

The mechanism involves nucleophilic attack on the \(\delta+\) carbon.

10.3 Reaction Mechanisms (HL)

Understanding mechanisms explains HOW reactions occur at the molecular level, showing bond breaking/forming and electron movement.

Curly arrows: Show movement of electron pairs

  • Full arrow: movement of electron pair
  • Half arrow: movement of single electron (radicals)

10.3.1 Nucleophilic Substitution Mechanisms

SN2 Mechanism (Substitution, Nucleophilic, Bimolecular):

  • One-step mechanism with transition state
  • Nucleophile attacks from opposite side to leaving group (backside attack)
  • Results in inversion of configuration (Walden inversion)
  • Rate depends on both [substrate] and [nucleophile]: Rate = k[RX][Nu-]
  • Favored by: primary halogenoalkanes, strong nucleophiles, polar aprotic solvents

SN1 Mechanism (Substitution, Nucleophilic, Unimolecular):

  • Two-step mechanism with carbocation intermediate
  • Step 1 (slow): Leaving group departs, forming carbocation
  • Step 2 (fast): Nucleophile attacks carbocation
  • Rate depends only on [substrate]: Rate = k[RX]
  • Results in racemization (both enantiomers formed from chiral starting material)
  • Favored by: tertiary halogenoalkanes (stable carbocations), weak nucleophiles, polar protic solvents

Carbocation stability: 3° > 2° > 1° > methyl (due to hyperconjugation and inductive effects)

10.3.2 Electrophilic Addition to Alkenes

The pi electrons in C=C act as nucleophile, attacking electrophiles.

Addition of HBr (explains Markovnikov's rule):

Step 1: Pi electrons attack H+ → forms more stable carbocation
Step 2: Br- attacks carbocation → product

10.3.3 Electrophilic Substitution of Benzene

Benzene is unusually stable due to delocalized pi electron system (aromaticity). Undergoes substitution rather than addition to preserve aromatic stability.

Nitration (forming nitrobenzene):

Generating electrophile: \(HNO_3 + H_2SO_4 \rightarrow NO_2^+ + HSO_4^- + H_2O\)

Overall: \(C_6H_6 + HNO_3 \xrightarrow{H_2SO_4} C_6H_5NO_2 + H_2O\)

10.3.4 Elimination Reactions

Compete with substitution reactions of halogenoalkanes, producing alkenes.

E2 Mechanism (Elimination, Bimolecular):

  • One-step concerted mechanism
  • Base abstracts proton while C-X bond breaks simultaneously
  • Rate = k[RX][Base]
  • Favored by: strong bases, higher temperatures

E1 Mechanism (Elimination, Unimolecular):

  • Two-step mechanism via carbocation
  • Step 1: Leaving group departs → carbocation
  • Step 2: Base removes proton from adjacent carbon → alkene
  • Rate = k[RX]
  • Favored by: tertiary substrates, weak bases, higher temperatures

Zaitsev's rule: Major product is the more substituted (more stable) alkene.

10.4 Synthetic Routes (HL)

Organic synthesis involves converting one compound into another through reaction sequences.

Key Functional Group Interconversions:

  1. Alkene → Alkane: Hydrogenation (H2, Ni catalyst)
  2. Alkene → Dihaloalkane: Addition of X2
  3. Alkene → Halogenoalkane: Addition of HX (Markovnikov)
  4. Alkene → Alcohol: Hydration (H2O, H3PO4)
  5. Halogenoalkane → Alcohol: Nucleophilic substitution (OH-, heat)
  6. Halogenoalkane → Amine: Reaction with NH3
  7. Halogenoalkane → Nitrile: Reaction with CN- (increases carbon chain)
  8. Halogenoalkane → Alkene: Elimination (strong base, heat)
  9. Alcohol → Halogenoalkane: Reaction with HX or PCl5
  10. Primary alcohol → Aldehyde: Oxidation with immediate distillation
  11. Primary alcohol → Carboxylic acid: Oxidation with reflux
  12. Secondary alcohol → Ketone: Oxidation
  13. Aldehyde → Carboxylic acid: Oxidation
  14. Aldehyde → Primary alcohol: Reduction (LiAlH4 or NaBH4)
  15. Ketone → Secondary alcohol: Reduction

Multi-Step Synthesis Strategy:

  1. Identify functional groups in starting material and target
  2. Determine required transformations
  3. Consider carbon chain length changes
  4. Plan reaction sequence
  5. Consider protecting groups if necessary

Retrosynthetic Analysis:

Work backwards from target molecule:

  1. Identify key bonds to disconnect
  2. Choose disconnections that simplify structure
  3. Ensure synthons correspond to available reagents
  4. Write forward synthesis

10.5 Stereochemistry (HL)

Stereochemistry studies the 3D arrangement of atoms in molecules.

10.5.1 Types of Isomerism

Structural Isomers (same molecular formula, different connectivity):

  1. Chain isomers: Different carbon skeleton arrangements
  2. Position isomers: Functional group at different positions
  3. Functional group isomers: Different functional groups

Stereoisomers (same structural formula, different spatial arrangement):

  1. Geometric isomers (E-Z isomers): Require restricted rotation (C=C double bond or ring)
  2. Optical isomers (enantiomers): Require chiral center (carbon with 4 different groups)

10.5.2 Cahn-Ingold-Prelog (CIP) Priority Rules

Used to assign R/S configuration to chiral centers:

  1. Assign priorities (1-4) to groups bonded to chiral center based on atomic number
  2. Orient molecule with lowest priority (4) pointing away
  3. Trace path from 1 → 2 → 3:
    • Clockwise = (R) (rectus, right)
    • Counterclockwise = (S) (sinister, left)

10.5.3 E-Z Nomenclature

For geometric isomers, CIP rules determine E vs Z:

  • Same side = (Z) (zusammen, together)
  • Opposite sides = (E) (entgegen, opposite)

10.5.4 Optical Activity

Optical activity: Ability to rotate plane of polarized light

  • Dextrorotatory (+): Rotates clockwise
  • Levorotatory (-): Rotates counterclockwise

Enantiomer properties:

  • Identical physical properties (bp, mp, density, solubility)
  • Identical chemical properties in achiral environments
  • Different: optical activity (equal magnitude, opposite direction)
  • Different: biological activity (enzymes are chiral)

Racemic mixture: 50:50 mixture of enantiomers, optically inactive (rotations cancel)

Real-World Applications

Pharmaceutical Industry: Stereochemistry is critical in drug development. Ibuprofen's (S)-enantiomer is the active pain reliever; the (R)-form is inactive.

Polymer Industry: Addition polymerization of alkenes produces materials used daily: polyethylene, polypropylene, polystyrene, and PVC.

Petroleum Industry: Free radical substitution and catalytic cracking convert alkanes from crude oil into useful products.

Biofuels: Ethanol production from fermentation and its combustion properties make it a renewable fuel additive.

Green Chemistry: Retrosynthetic analysis helps design efficient syntheses with fewer steps, reducing waste and energy consumption.

Common Misconceptions

  1. All molecules with chiral centers are optically active - FALSE. Meso compounds have chiral centers but are optically inactive due to internal symmetry.
  2. Tertiary carbocations are more reactive than primary - Misleading. They're more STABLE, not more reactive.
  3. Alkanes don't react at all - FALSE. They undergo combustion and free radical substitution.
  4. Markovnikov's rule is just memorization - FALSE. It's explained by carbocation stability.
  5. E and Z are the same as trans and cis - Not quite. E-Z is more general based on CIP priorities.
  6. SN1 produces one enantiomer - FALSE. Produces racemic mixture due to planar carbocation.
  7. All oxidation reactions use oxygen - FALSE. Oxidation means increasing oxygen OR decreasing hydrogen content.
  8. Geometric isomers occur only with C=C bonds - FALSE. Also occur in cyclic compounds.
  9. Primary alcohols always give aldehydes when oxidized - Only with immediate distillation; otherwise form carboxylic acids.
  10. Enantiomers have different chemical properties - Mostly FALSE. They react identically with achiral reagents.

Practice Problems

Problem 1
Name the following compound using IUPAC nomenclature: CH3CH2CH(CH3)CH2CH(Cl)CH3

Solution:

Step 1: Identify longest chain = 6 carbons (hexane)
Step 2: Identify functional group = Cl (chloro-), highest priority determines numbering
Step 3: Number from end nearest to Cl: positions 2 (Cl) and 4 (CH3)
Step 4: Alphabetical order: chloro before methyl

Answer: 2-chloro-4-methylhexane

Explanation:

The chloro group takes priority in numbering over methyl substituents. Always number to give functional groups the lowest numbers, then list substituents alphabetically regardless of position numbers.

Problem 2
Compound X has molecular formula C4H10O. It can be oxidized to give compound Y, which gives a positive Tollens' test. Identify X and Y, and write the oxidation equation.

Solution:

Step 1: Tollens' test positive → Y is an aldehyde (not ketone)
Step 2: Aldehyde from oxidation → X is a primary alcohol
Step 3: C4H10O primary alcohol = butan-1-ol: CH3CH2CH2CH2OH
Step 4: Oxidation product Y = butanal: CH3CH2CH2CHO
Step 5: Equation: CH3CH2CH2CH2OH + [O] → CH3CH2CH2CHO + H2O

Answer: X = butan-1-ol, Y = butanal

Explanation:

Tollens' reagent (silver mirror test) distinguishes aldehydes from ketones - only aldehydes give positive result. Working backwards, since aldehydes come from oxidizing primary alcohols, we can deduce X. Butan-2-ol would give a ketone (butan-2-one), which wouldn't pass Tollens' test.

Problem 3
Draw the mechanism for the SN2 reaction between bromoethane and hydroxide ion. Show the stereochemistry if the starting material were (R)-2-bromobutane instead.

Solution:

For bromoethane (CH3CH2Br + OH-):
Step 1: Draw OH- approaching from opposite side to Br
Step 2: Show transition state: [HO---CH2CH3---Br]- with partial bonds
Step 3: Products: CH3CH2OH + Br-
Curly arrow from O lone pair to carbon, curly arrow from C-Br bond to Br

For (R)-2-bromobutane:
- SN2 occurs with backside attack
- Results in inversion of configuration (Walden inversion)
- Product: (S)-butan-2-ol

Stereochemistry: (R) → (S) due to backside attack

Explanation:

SN2 is one-step with transition state where nucleophile attacks from side opposite to leaving group. This backside attack causes inversion of stereochemistry. If you start with R configuration, you end with S configuration (and vice versa).

Problem 4
Explain why but-2-ene can exist as geometric isomers but but-1-ene cannot.

Solution:

But-2-ene: CH3CH=CHCH3
- Double bond between C2 and C3
- C2 has: H and CH3 groups
- C3 has: H and CH3 groups
- Different groups on each carbon of double bond
- Can have E-isomer (CH3 groups opposite) and Z-isomer (CH3 groups same side)

But-1-ene: CH2=CHCH2CH3
- Double bond between C1 and C2
- C1 has: two H atoms (identical groups)
- Even though rotation is restricted, having two identical groups means no geometric isomers possible

Requirements for geometric isomerism:
1. Restricted rotation (double bond or ring)
2. Each carbon of double bond must have two different groups

Explanation:

Students often think restricted rotation alone is sufficient, but you need different groups on each carbon. When one carbon has two identical groups, geometric isomerism is impossible.

Problem 5
A compound with formula C5H12 is reacted with chlorine in the presence of UV light. Three monochloro isomers are formed. Identify the original compound and explain your reasoning.

Solution:

Step 1: C5H12 is an alkane (saturated hydrocarbon)
Step 2: Possible isomers: pentane, 2-methylbutane, 2,2-dimethylpropane

Step 3: Count possible monochloro products:

Pentane (CH3CH2CH2CH2CH3):
- Cl at C1: CH2ClCH2CH2CH2CH3 (also same as C5 by symmetry)
- Cl at C2: CH3CHClCH2CH2CH3 (also same as C4)
- Cl at C3: CH3CH2CHClCH2CH3
= 3 different monochloro products ✓

2-methylbutane would give 4 products
2,2-dimethylpropane would give 1 product

Answer: The original compound is pentane (CH3CH2CH2CH2CH3)

Explanation:

Free radical substitution can occur at any C-H bond, but symmetrical positions give the same product. Pentane has a plane of symmetry, making C1=C5 and C2=C4 equivalent. This gives exactly three distinct products.

Problem 6
Design a synthesis of propanone starting from propene. Show all steps, reagents, and conditions.

Solution:

Target: Propanone (CH3COCH3) - a ketone
Starting material: Propene (CH3CH=CH2)

Analysis: Ketones come from oxidizing secondary alcohols. Need to convert propene to secondary alcohol first.

Step 1: Hydration of propene
CH3CH=CH2 + H2O → CH3CH(OH)CH3
Reagents: H3PO4 catalyst, 300°C, 60 atm
Product: Propan-2-ol (secondary alcohol)
Note: Follows Markovnikov's rule - OH adds to more substituted carbon

Step 2: Oxidation of propan-2-ol
CH3CH(OH)CH3 + [O] → CH3COCH3 + H2O
Reagents: Acidified potassium dichromate (K2Cr2O7/H+)
Conditions: Heat under reflux
Observation: Orange dichromate → green Cr3+
Product: Propanone

Overall: Propene → Propan-2-ol → Propanone

Explanation:

This synthesis tests understanding of alkene reactions and alcohol oxidation. Key points: (1) Markovnikov addition gives secondary alcohol not primary, (2) Secondary alcohols oxidize to ketones and cannot be further oxidized.

Problem 7
A student performs a test to distinguish between hexane and hex-1-ene by adding bromine water to each. Explain what would be observed and the chemistry behind it.

Solution:

Test: Add bromine water (Br2(aq)) to samples of both hydrocarbons

Observations:
Hexane (C6H14): Orange-brown color persists (no visible change)
Hex-1-ene (C6H12): Orange-brown color disappears/decolorizes rapidly

Chemistry behind observations:

Hexane (alkane):
- Only single C-C and C-H bonds (saturated)
- Strong, non-polar bonds
- Br2 does not react at room temperature without UV light
- No reaction occurs, bromine remains in solution (orange-brown)

Hex-1-ene (alkene):
- Contains C=C double bond (unsaturated)
- Pi bond is weaker and more reactive
- Br2 undergoes electrophilic addition across double bond
- Reaction: CH2=CHCH2CH2CH2CH3 + Br2 → CH2BrCHBrCH2CH2CH2CH3
- Bromine consumed, so color disappears
- Product is 1,2-dibromohexane (colorless)

Conclusion: Bromine water test distinguishes saturated from unsaturated hydrocarbons. Decolorization indicates C=C present.

Explanation:

This is a standard practical test in IB Chemistry. The key is explaining WHY the reaction occurs with alkenes but not alkanes - it's about the reactivity of the pi bond vs sigma bonds.

Exam Tips

  • Master skeletal formulas - most IB exam questions use them. Practice converting between full, condensed, and skeletal representations.
  • When naming compounds, always identify the functional group first as it determines numbering direction. Number to give functional groups lowest numbers, not substituents.
  • For mechanism questions, show ALL curly arrows, lone pairs, and charges. Half-marks are often lost for incomplete mechanisms even if the overall idea is correct.
  • Distinguish between SN1 and SN2 by substrate: 1° favors SN2, 3° favors SN1, 2° gives mixtures. Temperature and nucleophile strength also matter.
  • In oxidation questions, state the reagent (acidified K2Cr2O7 or KMnO4) and conditions (distill for aldehyde, reflux for carboxylic acid). Color change observations earn marks.
  • For isomerism questions, always check for geometric isomers (restricted rotation + different groups on each carbon) and optical isomers (chiral center = 4 different groups).
  • Markovnikov's rule: explain it using carbocation stability, don't just memorize. More stable carbocation forms → determines regioselectivity.
  • In synthesis questions, show all steps with reagents and conditions. Don't skip steps - each transformation must be chemically valid.
  • When asked to compare compounds, make direct comparisons. Don't just describe each one separately - explicitly state how they differ.
  • Learn the priority order of functional groups for nomenclature: COOH > CHO > C=O > OH > C=C. This determines the suffix and numbering.
  • For stereochemistry, practice drawing 3D structures with wedges (out of plane) and dashes (into plane). Essential for showing R/S configurations clearly.
  • Bromine water test: know what it tests for (unsaturation), what is observed (decolorization), and write the equation for the addition reaction.
  • In elimination vs substitution questions, always mention temperature - this is often the deciding factor and earns easy marks.
  • When using Cahn-Ingold-Prelog rules, work systematically. If you can't decide priority at first atom, move to second atoms outward. Show your working.
  • For optical isomers, remember: enantiomers have opposite optical rotation but otherwise identical properties. Different biological activity is the key consequence.
  • Polymerization: show repeating unit in brackets with 'n' subscript. Make sure the bonds correctly show continuation of the chain.
  • Common error: writing aldehyde oxidation as separate reaction. Remember primary alcohols can go directly to carboxylic acids with excess oxidizer and reflux.
  • Zaitsev's rule in elimination: more substituted alkene is more stable and thus major product. Explain using hyperconjugation if asked.
  • For benzene reactions, always mention the catalyst (AlCl3, FeBr3, or H2SO4) - these are essential and marks are lost if omitted.
  • In Data-Based Questions, read uncertainties carefully and quote answers to appropriate significant figures based on the data precision.
