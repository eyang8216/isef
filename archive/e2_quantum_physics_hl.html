Key Definitions

Photoelectric Effect
The emission of electrons from a metal surface when electromagnetic radiation above a threshold frequency is incident upon it. Einstein's explanation: light consists of photons with energy \(E = hf\), where each photon can eject one electron if \(hf \geq \phi\) (work function).
Work Function (φ)
The minimum energy required to remove an electron from the surface of a material. Measured in joules (J) or electron volts (eV). \(\phi = hf_0\) where \(f_0\) is the threshold frequency.
de Broglie Wavelength
The wavelength associated with any moving particle, given by \(\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{mv}\), where \(h\) is Planck's constant, \(p\) is momentum, \(m\) is mass, and \(v\) is velocity. This establishes wave-particle duality for matter.
Wave Function (ψ)
A mathematical function that describes the quantum state of a particle. The square of its magnitude \(|\psi|^2\) gives the probability density of finding the particle at a particular location. It is the solution to the Schrödinger equation.
Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle
A fundamental limit on the precision with which certain pairs of physical properties can be simultaneously known. For position and momentum: \(\Delta x \Delta p \geq \frac{h}{4\pi}\). For energy and time: \(\Delta E \Delta t \geq \frac{h}{4\pi}\).
Quantum Tunnelling
A quantum mechanical phenomenon where a particle can pass through a potential barrier that it classically could not surmount. The probability depends on barrier width, height, and particle energy, described by transmission coefficient \(T\).
Pair Production
The creation of a particle and its antiparticle from energy (typically a photon), following \(E = 2m_0c^2\) minimum energy requirement. Example: photon → electron + positron. Requires minimum photon energy of 1.022 MeV.
Pair Annihilation
The process where a particle and its antiparticle collide and annihilate, converting their rest mass and kinetic energy into photons. For electron-positron annihilation at rest, two 0.511 MeV photons are produced.

Introduction: The Quantum Revolution

Classical physics, developed through the 19th century, successfully described motion, forces, waves, and thermodynamics. However, by 1900, several experimental observations defied classical explanation: blackbody radiation spectra, the photoelectric effect, and atomic stability. These puzzles led to quantum physics, a revolutionary framework where energy is quantized, particles exhibit wave properties, and measurement fundamentally affects outcomes.

Topic E2 extends your SL foundation by exploring the mathematical and conceptual depth of quantum mechanics. You'll learn how Einstein explained the photoelectric effect with photons, how de Broglie unified particle and wave descriptions, how the Bohr model quantizes atomic energy levels, and how the wave function and uncertainty principle reveal the probabilistic nature of reality.

Prerequisites: You should be comfortable with:

  • Wave properties (wavelength, frequency, interference, diffraction) from Topic C
  • Energy, momentum, and kinetic energy from Topics A and B
  • Atomic structure basics (nucleus, electrons, energy levels) from Topic E1
  • Electromagnetic spectrum and photon energy \(E = hf\) from SL content

Real-world significance: Quantum physics underpins modern technology including semiconductors, lasers, MRI scanners, electron microscopes, quantum computers, and photovoltaic cells. Understanding quantum mechanics is essential for nuclear physics, particle physics, and materials science.

The Photoelectric Effect (E.2.1)

Classical Wave Prediction vs. Experimental Reality

When light strikes a metal surface, electrons may be emitted—this is the photoelectric effect. Classical wave theory predicted:

  1. Emission should occur for any frequency if intensity is sufficient
  2. Electron kinetic energy should increase with light intensity
  3. There should be a time delay while electrons absorb enough energy

Experiments (particularly by Lenard and Millikan) showed:

  1. No emission occurs below a threshold frequency \(f_0\), regardless of intensity
  2. Maximum electron kinetic energy depends only on frequency, not intensity
  3. Emission is instantaneous (< 10⁻⁹ s)
  4. Intensity affects only the rate of emission (number of electrons per second)

Einstein's Photon Explanation (1905)

Einstein proposed that light consists of discrete packets (photons), each with energy:

$$E_{\text{photon}} = hf$$

where \(h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\) J·s is Planck's constant and \(f\) is frequency.

When a photon strikes the metal, it interacts with a single electron in an all-or-nothing process. If the photon energy exceeds the work function \(\phi\) (the binding energy of the electron to the metal), the electron is ejected with kinetic energy:

$$E_k = hf - \phi$$

This is Einstein's photoelectric equation. The threshold frequency \(f_0\) occurs when \(E_k = 0\):

$$hf_0 = \phi \quad \Rightarrow \quad f_0 = \frac{\phi}{h}$$

Below this frequency, no individual photon has sufficient energy to liberate an electron.

Stopping Potential

In photoelectric experiments, a reverse potential difference \(V_s\) (stopping potential) is applied to prevent the most energetic electrons from reaching the collector. At the stopping potential:

$$eV_s = E_{k,\text{max}} = hf - \phi$$

Plotting \(V_s\) versus \(f\) yields a straight line with slope \(h/e\) and x-intercept \(f_0\).

Common Misconceptions:

  • Intensity does NOT affect electron energy, only emission rate
  • Each photon ejects at most one electron (one-to-one interaction)
  • Work function varies by material and surface condition
  • The equation gives maximum kinetic energy; electrons deeper in the metal emerge with less energy

Matter Waves and de Broglie Wavelength (E.2.2)

Wave-Particle Duality for Matter

In 1924, Louis de Broglie proposed that if waves (light) can behave as particles (photons), then particles should exhibit wave properties. He hypothesized that any particle with momentum \(p\) has an associated wavelength:

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{mv}$$

For non-relativistic particles, this is the de Broglie wavelength. For relativistic particles or photons, use \(p = E/c\).

Electron Diffraction Evidence

Davisson and Germer (1927) experimentally confirmed de Broglie's hypothesis by diffracting electrons from a nickel crystal. The electrons produced an interference pattern characteristic of waves, with spacing consistent with:

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2m_e eV}}$$

for electrons accelerated through potential difference \(V\). This established that electrons, previously considered purely particles, exhibit wave behavior.

Implications:

  • All matter has wave properties, but \(\lambda\) is typically observable only for microscopic particles
  • For macroscopic objects, \(\lambda\) is so small (due to large mass) that wave effects are negligible
  • Electron microscopes exploit electron wavelengths much shorter than visible light for superior resolution

Calculating de Broglie Wavelengths

For a particle with kinetic energy \(E_k = \frac{1}{2}mv^2\):

$$p = \sqrt{2mE_k}$$

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2mE_k}}$$

For an electron accelerated through potential \(V\):

$$E_k = eV$$

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2m_e eV}} = \frac{1.23 \times 10^{-9}}{\sqrt{V}} \text{ m (with V in volts)}$$

When is wave behavior observable?

Wave effects (diffraction, interference) become significant when \(\lambda\) is comparable to the size of obstacles or apertures. For electrons with \(E_k \sim 100\) eV, \(\lambda \sim 0.1\) nm, comparable to atomic spacing in crystals—hence observable diffraction patterns.

Pair Production and Annihilation (E.2.3)

Mass-Energy Equivalence in Quantum Processes

Einstein's \(E = mc^2\) predicts that energy can convert to mass and vice versa. In quantum physics, this occurs through pair production and pair annihilation.

Pair Production

A high-energy photon can spontaneously convert into a particle-antiparticle pair, typically an electron (\(e^-\)) and positron (\(e^+\)):

$$\gamma \rightarrow e^- + e^+$$

Conservation of energy requires:

$$E_{\gamma} = 2m_e c^2 + E_{k,e^-} + E_{k,e^+}$$

The minimum (threshold) photon energy occurs when both particles are created at rest:

$$E_{\gamma,\text{min}} = 2m_e c^2 = 2 \times 0.511 \text{ MeV} = 1.022 \text{ MeV}$$

This corresponds to a photon frequency:

$$f_{\text{min}} = \frac{2m_e c^2}{h} = 2.47 \times 10^{20} \text{ Hz}$$

Pair production cannot occur in vacuum alone due to momentum conservation—it requires a nearby nucleus to absorb recoil momentum.

Pair Annihilation

When a particle meets its antiparticle, they annihilate, converting their mass and kinetic energy into photons:

$$e^- + e^+ \rightarrow 2\gamma$$

For an electron-positron pair at rest, two photons of equal energy are produced (to conserve momentum):

$$E_{\gamma} = m_e c^2 = 0.511 \text{ MeV each}$$

These photons travel in opposite directions. If the particles have kinetic energy, the total energy is shared among the photons, and their energies need not be equal.

Applications:

  • Positron Emission Tomography (PET): Positrons from radioactive decay annihilate with electrons, producing detectable 511 keV photon pairs
  • Gamma-ray astronomy: Observing annihilation radiation from matter-antimatter interactions
  • Particle accelerators: Creating exotic particles through high-energy collisions

Quantization of Angular Momentum: The Bohr Model (E.2.4)

Classical Instability of Atoms

Rutherford's nuclear model placed electrons in orbits around the nucleus, but classical electromagnetism predicts that accelerating charges radiate energy. Orbiting electrons should spiral into the nucleus in ~10⁻¹¹ s, yet atoms are stable. Atomic spectra also show discrete lines, not continuous radiation.

Bohr's Quantum Postulates (1913)

Niels Bohr resolved this by proposing:

1. Stationary states: Electrons occupy specific orbits with quantized angular momentum:

$$L = n\frac{h}{2\pi} = n\hbar$$

where \(n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots\) is the principal quantum number and \(\hbar = h/(2\pi) = 1.055 \times 10^{-34}\) J·s.

2. No radiation in stationary states: Electrons do not radiate while in these allowed orbits.

3. Quantum jumps: Electrons transition between energy levels by absorbing or emitting photons:

$$E_{\gamma} = E_i - E_f = hf$$

Deriving Energy Levels for Hydrogen

For an electron in orbit \(n\), balancing centripetal and electrostatic forces:

$$\frac{mv^2}{r} = \frac{ke^2}{r^2}$$

With quantized angular momentum \(L = mvr = n\hbar\):

$$r_n = \frac{n^2\hbar^2}{m_e ke^2} = n^2 a_0$$

where \(a_0 = 0.529 \times 10^{-10}\) m is the Bohr radius (ground state radius).

The total energy (kinetic + potential) is:

$$E_n = -\frac{m_e k^2 e^4}{2\hbar^2 n^2} = -\frac{13.6 \text{ eV}}{n^2}$$

The negative sign indicates bound states. The ground state (\(n=1\)) has \(E_1 = -13.6\) eV.

Spectral Lines

Transitions between levels produce spectral lines:

$$\frac{1}{\lambda} = R_H \left(\frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2}\right)$$

where \(R_H = 1.097 \times 10^7\) m⁻¹ is the Rydberg constant. Series include:

  • Lyman series: \(n_f = 1\) (UV)
  • Balmer series: \(n_f = 2\) (visible)
  • Paschen series: \(n_f = 3\) (IR)

Limitations of the Bohr Model:

  • Only works for hydrogen-like atoms (one electron)
  • Cannot explain fine structure, Zeeman effect, or chemical bonding
  • Ad hoc quantization rule; no justification from first principles
  • Replaced by quantum mechanics (Schrödinger equation), which provides wave function descriptions

The Wave Function (E.2.5)

From Particles to Probability Waves

In quantum mechanics, particles are described not by definite positions and momenta, but by a wave function \(\psi(x,t)\), a complex-valued function that encodes all information about the system. The wave function is the solution to the Schrödinger equation.

Born's Probability Interpretation

Max Born (1926) proposed that \(|\psi(x,t)|^2\) represents the probability density of finding the particle at position \(x\) at time \(t\). Specifically:

$$P(x) = |\psi(x)|^2$$

The probability of finding the particle in an interval \([x_1, x_2]\) is:

$$P(x_1 < x < x_2) = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} |\psi(x)|^2 \, dx$$

Normalization

Since the particle must be somewhere, the total probability is:

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |\psi(x)|^2 \, dx = 1$$

This is the normalization condition.

Physical Interpretation:

  • The wave function itself (\(\psi\)) is not directly observable—it's complex
  • Only \(|\psi|^2\) has physical meaning (probability density)
  • Before measurement, the particle exists in a superposition of states
  • Measurement "collapses" the wave function to a definite outcome
  • Repeated measurements on identically prepared systems yield a probability distribution matching \(|\psi|^2\)

Example: Particle in a Box

For a particle confined to \(0 < x < L\) with infinite potential walls, the allowed wave functions are:

$$\psi_n(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{L}} \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)$$

with energies:

$$E_n = \frac{n^2 h^2}{8mL^2}$$

The probability density \(|\psi_n|^2\) shows nodes (zero probability) and antinodes (high probability) within the box.

Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle (E.2.6)

Fundamental Limits on Measurement

The uncertainty principle, discovered by Werner Heisenberg in 1927, states that certain pairs of physical quantities (complementary variables) cannot both be known with arbitrary precision simultaneously.

Position-Momentum Uncertainty

$$\Delta x \Delta p \geq \frac{h}{4\pi} = \frac{\hbar}{2}$$

where:

  • \(\Delta x\) is the uncertainty (standard deviation) in position
  • \(\Delta p\) is the uncertainty in momentum
  • \(\hbar = h/(2\pi) = 1.055 \times 10^{-34}\) J·s

This is NOT due to experimental imperfection—it's a fundamental property of quantum systems. The more precisely you know position, the less precisely you can know momentum, and vice versa.

Energy-Time Uncertainty

$$\Delta E \Delta t \geq \frac{h}{4\pi} = \frac{\hbar}{2}$$

where:

  • \(\Delta E\) is the uncertainty in energy
  • \(\Delta t\) is the time duration of the measurement or lifetime of the state

This relation allows virtual particles to exist briefly, violating energy conservation for time \(\Delta t \sim \hbar/(\Delta E)\).

Physical Origin

The uncertainty principle arises from wave-particle duality:

  • A well-localized wave packet (small \(\Delta x\)) requires many frequency components, giving large momentum spread (\(\Delta p\))
  • A pure sine wave (definite momentum, \(\Delta p = 0\)) extends over all space (\(\Delta x = \infty\))

Implications

1. Ground state energy: Particles cannot have zero energy in bound states. In an infinite well of width \(L\), \(\Delta x \sim L\), so \(\Delta p \geq \hbar/(2L)\), giving minimum kinetic energy \(E_{\text{min}} \sim \hbar^2/(2mL^2)\).

2. Atomic stability: Electrons cannot spiral into the nucleus. If confined to nuclear dimensions (\(\Delta x \sim 10^{-15}\) m), \(\Delta p\) would be enormous, giving kinetic energy far exceeding typical binding energies.

3. Spectral line width: Excited atomic states with lifetime \(\tau\) have energy uncertainty \(\Delta E \sim \hbar/(2\tau)\), producing natural line broadening \(\Delta f \sim 1/(2\pi\tau)\).

Quantum Tunnelling (E.2.7)

Classical Forbidden, Quantum Allowed

Classically, a particle with energy \(E\) cannot pass through a potential barrier of height \(V_0 > E\)—it simply doesn't have enough energy. Quantum mechanically, the wave function penetrates into and sometimes through the barrier. This is quantum tunnelling.

Wave Function in a Barrier

Consider a particle with energy \(E\) encountering a rectangular potential barrier (height \(V_0\), width \(a\)):

  • Region I (\(x < 0\)): \(\psi_I = A e^{ikx} + B e^{-ikx}\) (incident + reflected waves, \(k = \sqrt{2mE}/\hbar\))
  • Region II (barrier, \(0 < x < a\)): \(\psi_{II} = C e^{\kappa x} + D e^{-\kappa x}\) (decaying exponentials, \(\kappa = \sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}/\hbar\))
  • Region III (\(x > a\)): \(\psi_{III} = F e^{ikx}\) (transmitted wave)

Inside the barrier, the wave function decays exponentially but does not vanish. If the barrier is thin enough, \(\psi\) has non-zero amplitude on the far side, allowing transmission.

Transmission Coefficient

The probability of tunnelling through the barrier is:

$$T \approx e^{-2\kappa a} = \exp\left(-\frac{2a}{\hbar}\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}\right)$$

for \(V_0 \gg E\) and thick barriers. This shows:

  • \(T\) decreases exponentially with barrier width \(a\)
  • \(T\) decreases with barrier height \((V_0 - E)\)
  • \(T\) increases for lighter particles (smaller \(m\))

Applications and Examples

1. Alpha decay: Alpha particles tunnel through the Coulomb barrier of the nucleus. The barrier width depends on nuclear radius and height depends on Coulomb repulsion. Tunnelling probability determines decay half-life.

2. Scanning Tunnelling Microscope (STM): A sharp metal tip is brought near a conducting surface. Electrons tunnel across the gap (typically ~1 nm). Tunnelling current is extremely sensitive to tip-surface distance:

$$I \propto e^{-2\kappa d}$$

By scanning the tip and maintaining constant current, atomic-resolution surface images are obtained.

3. Tunnel diodes: Semiconductor devices exploiting quantum tunnelling for ultrafast switching.

4. Nuclear fusion: Protons in the Sun's core tunnel through the Coulomb barrier to fuse, despite having kinetic energies far below the classical barrier height (~1 MeV vs ~10 keV thermal energy).

Practice Problems

Problem 1 (Photoelectric Effect): Light of wavelength 400 nm is incident on a potassium surface with work function 2.24 eV. (a) Calculate the maximum kinetic energy of emitted electrons in eV. (b) Determine the stopping potential. (c) Find the threshold wavelength for potassium. (\(h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\) J·s, \(c = 3.00 \times 10^8\) m/s, \(e = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\) C)

(a) Maximum kinetic energy:
First, find photon energy: \(E = hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{(6.63 \times 10^{-34})(3.00 \times 10^8)}{400 \times 10^{-9}} = 4.97 \times 10^{-19}\) J

Convert to eV: \(E = \frac{4.97 \times 10^{-19}}{1.60 \times 10^{-19}} = 3.11\) eV

Using Einstein's equation: \(E_k = hf - \phi = 3.11 - 2.24 = \mathbf{0.87 \text{ eV}}\)

(b) Stopping potential:
\(eV_s = E_k \Rightarrow V_s = \mathbf{0.87 \text{ V}}\)

(c) Threshold wavelength:
\(\phi = hf_0 = \frac{hc}{\lambda_0}\)
\(\lambda_0 = \frac{hc}{\phi} = \frac{(6.63 \times 10^{-34})(3.00 \times 10^8)}{2.24 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19}} = 5.55 \times 10^{-7}\) m = 555 nm

Problem 2 (de Broglie Wavelength): An electron is accelerated from rest through a potential difference of 150 V. (a) Calculate its de Broglie wavelength. (b) Compare this to the wavelength of a proton accelerated through the same potential. (\(m_e = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\) kg, \(m_p = 1.67 \times 10^{-27}\) kg, \(e = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\) C, \(h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\) J·s)

(a) Electron wavelength:
Using the formula: \(\lambda = \frac{1.23 \times 10^{-9}}{\sqrt{V}} = \frac{1.23 \times 10^{-9}}{\sqrt{150}} = 1.00 \times 10^{-10}\) m = 0.100 nm

(b) Proton wavelength:
Same kinetic energy, but \(m_p = 1836 m_e\)
\(p = \sqrt{2m_p E_k} = \sqrt{1836} \times p_e = 42.8 \times p_e\)
\(\lambda_p = \frac{h}{p_p} = \frac{\lambda_e}{42.8} = \frac{1.00 \times 10^{-10}}{42.8} = 2.34 \times 10^{-12}\) m = 0.00234 nm

The proton's wavelength is much shorter due to its larger mass.

Problem 3 (Pair Production): A gamma-ray photon with energy 2.50 MeV undergoes pair production near a heavy nucleus. (a) Is pair production possible? (b) If so, calculate the total kinetic energy of the electron-positron pair created. (c) If the particles share this kinetic energy equally, find the speed of each particle. (\(m_e c^2 = 0.511\) MeV)

(a) Possibility check:
Minimum energy for pair production: \(E_{\text{min}} = 2m_e c^2 = 2(0.511) = 1.022\) MeV
Since \(E_{\gamma} = 2.50\) MeV \(> 1.022\) MeV, pair production is possible.

(b) Total kinetic energy:
By energy conservation: \(E_{\gamma} = 2m_e c^2 + E_k^{\text{total}}\)
\(E_k^{\text{total}} = 2.50 - 1.022 = \mathbf{1.48 \text{ MeV}}\)

(c) Speed of each particle:
Each particle gets: \(E_k = 1.478/2 = 0.739\) MeV
Total energy of each: \(E_{\text{total}} = 0.739 + 0.511 = 1.250\) MeV
Using \(E = \gamma m_e c^2\): \(\gamma = 1.250/0.511 = 2.446\)
\(v = c\sqrt{1-1/\gamma^2} = 0.913c = \mathbf{2.74 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}}\)

Problem 4 (Bohr Model): A hydrogen atom is in the \(n = 4\) state. (a) Calculate the orbital radius. (b) Find the electron's speed. (c) Determine the energy of the photon emitted when the electron transitions to the \(n = 2\) state. (d) Identify which spectral series this transition belongs to. (\(a_0 = 0.529 \times 10^{-10}\) m, \(E_1 = -13.6\) eV)

(a) Orbital radius:
\(r_n = n^2 a_0 = 4^2 (0.529 \times 10^{-10}) = 16(0.529 \times 10^{-10}) = 8.46 \times 10^{-10}\) m = 0.846 nm

(b) Electron speed:
\(v = \frac{\hbar}{n m_e a_0} = \frac{v_1}{4}\) where \(v_1 = 2.19 \times 10^6\) m/s
\(v_4 = 5.48 \times 10^5\) m/s

(c) Photon energy:
\(E_4 = \frac{-13.6}{16} = -0.85\) eV
\(E_2 = \frac{-13.6}{4} = -3.40\) eV
\(E_{\gamma} = E_4 - E_2 = -0.85 - (-3.40) = \mathbf{2.55 \text{ eV}}\)
This corresponds to \(\lambda = 486\) nm (blue-green light)

(d) Spectral series:
Since \(n_f = 2\), this belongs to the Balmer series (visible light, H-β line).

Problem 5 (Uncertainty Principle): An electron is confined to a region of width \(\Delta x = 0.10\) nm (approximately atomic size). (a) Estimate the minimum uncertainty in its momentum. (b) Estimate the minimum kinetic energy. (c) Explain why this prevents the electron from falling into the nucleus. (\(\hbar = 1.055 \times 10^{-34}\) J·s, \(m_e = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\) kg)

(a) Momentum uncertainty:
Using the uncertainty principle: \(\Delta x \Delta p \geq \frac{\hbar}{2}\)
\(\Delta p \geq \frac{\hbar}{2\Delta x} = \frac{1.055 \times 10^{-34}}{2(0.10 \times 10^{-9})} = \mathbf{5.28 \times 10^{-25} \text{ kg·m/s}}\)

(b) Minimum kinetic energy:
\(E_k \sim \frac{(\Delta p)^2}{2m_e} = \frac{(5.28 \times 10^{-25})^2}{2(9.11 \times 10^{-31})} = 1.53 \times 10^{-19}\) J ≈ 0.96 eV

(c) Nuclear collapse prevention:
If the electron were at the nucleus (\(\Delta x \sim 10^{-15}\) m):
\(\Delta p \geq \frac{1.055 \times 10^{-34}}{2(10^{-15})} = 5.28 \times 10^{-20}\) kg·m/s
\(E_k \sim 1.53 \times 10^{-9}\) J ≈ 10,000 MeV

This kinetic energy vastly exceeds typical atomic binding energies (~10 eV). The electron cannot be confined to nuclear dimensions because the required kinetic energy would far exceed the electrostatic attraction. Thus, the uncertainty principle enforces atomic size and stability.

Problem 6 (Quantum Tunnelling): An electron with energy \(E = 5.0\) eV approaches a rectangular potential barrier of height \(V_0 = 10.0\) eV and width \(a = 0.50\) nm. (a) Calculate the decay constant \(\kappa\) inside the barrier. (b) Estimate the transmission coefficient. (c) If \(10^{12}\) electrons per second strike the barrier, approximately how many tunnel through per second? (\(m_e = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\) kg, \(\hbar = 1.055 \times 10^{-34}\) J·s, \(e = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\) C)

(a) Decay constant:
\(\kappa = \frac{\sqrt{2m_e(V_0-E)}}{\hbar}\)
\(V_0 - E = 5.0\) eV \(= 8.0 \times 10^{-19}\) J
\(\kappa = \frac{\sqrt{2(9.11 \times 10^{-31})(8.0 \times 10^{-19})}}{1.055 \times 10^{-34}} = \mathbf{1.14 \times 10^{10} \text{ m}^{-1}}\)

(b) Transmission coefficient:
\(T \approx e^{-2\kappa a} = \exp[-2(1.14 \times 10^{10})(0.50 \times 10^{-9})]\)
\(T = e^{-11.4} = \mathbf{1.1 \times 10^{-5}}\) or 0.0011%

(c) Tunnelling rate:
Number tunnelling = (incident rate) × \(T\) = \((10^{12})(1.1 \times 10^{-5}) = \mathbf{1.1 \times 10^7 \text{ electrons/s}}\)
Approximately 11 million electrons per second tunnel through, despite having insufficient classical energy.

Exam Tips

Photoelectric effect: Remember that intensity affects the RATE of emission (number of electrons/s), while frequency affects electron ENERGY. Sketch \(V_s\) vs \(f\) graphs showing the threshold frequency and slope \(h/e\).
de Broglie wavelength: Always check if the particle is relativistic (compare kinetic energy to rest mass energy). For electrons, use the handy formula \(\lambda = 1.23\) nm/\(\sqrt{V}\)(volts) when accelerated from rest. For other particles, work from first principles.
Pair production/annihilation: Always check energy conservation AND momentum conservation. Pair production requires a nearby nucleus for momentum conservation—it cannot occur in empty space. In annihilation, two photons (not one) are produced for the same reason.
Bohr model: Know the key results by heart: ground state energy \(E_1 = -13.6\) eV, Bohr radius \(a_0 = 0.529\) Å, and the scaling laws (\(r_n \propto n^2\), \(E_n \propto 1/n^2\)). Be able to identify spectral series (Lyman, Balmer, Paschen) by the final state.
Wave function: Remember that \(\psi\) itself is not observable—only \(|\psi|^2\) has physical meaning as probability density. When finding probabilities, you must integrate \(|\psi|^2\) over the region of interest. Don't forget normalization (total probability = 1).
Uncertainty principle: The most common error is using = instead of ≥. The relation gives a LOWER BOUND, not an exact value. Use it for order-of-magnitude estimates in conceptual problems (atomic size, ground state energy). Don't confuse it with measurement error.
Quantum tunnelling: Transmission probability decreases exponentially with barrier width and height. Even tiny changes in width cause huge changes in transmission. Always express \(T\) in exponential form when comparing tunnelling probabilities.
Units and conversions: Master eV ↔ joules (1 eV = 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ J), energy ↔ wavelength (\(\lambda\)(nm) = 1240/\(E\)(eV)), and momentum ↔ wavelength. These conversions appear in nearly every quantum physics problem.
Problem-solving strategy: (1) Identify which quantum principle applies, (2) list given values and convert units, (3) choose the appropriate equation, (4) solve algebraically before substituting, (5) check if your answer is physically reasonable (orders of magnitude, units, signs).
Common mark losses: Not showing working, incorrect unit conversions, using approximate values when exact answers are required, not reading whether the question asks for wavelength or frequency, forgetting to square the wave function when finding probability, mixing up threshold frequency and incident frequency.
