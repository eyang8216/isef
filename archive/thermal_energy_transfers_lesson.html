Higher Level (HL)

Introduction to Thermal Energy Transfers

Thermal energy transfer is fundamental to understanding how energy moves through matter and between systems. This topic bridges microscopic molecular behavior with macroscopic thermodynamic properties, forming the foundation for topics in thermodynamics, climate science, engineering, and astrophysics.

Prerequisites: You should be comfortable with kinetic energy ($E_k = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$), potential energy, Newton's laws, and basic statistical concepts. Understanding that temperature is related to average kinetic energy of particles will be essential.

Topic Overview: Topic B1 explores three main areas: (1) the molecular model of matter and how it explains thermal properties, (2) energy storage and transfer through heat capacity and phase changes, and (3) mechanisms of heat transfer between systems. This topic is heavily tested through both data-based questions and conceptual problems requiring clear explanation of underlying principles.

Molecular Theory of Matter

The kinetic molecular theory provides a microscopic explanation for macroscopic thermal phenomena. Matter consists of particles (atoms or molecules) in constant random motion, and the properties we observe—temperature, pressure, phase—emerge from collective particle behavior.

The Three States of Matter

Solids: Particles are closely packed in regular geometric arrangements (crystal lattice). They vibrate about fixed equilibrium positions with relatively small amplitude. Strong intermolecular forces maintain structural rigidity. The potential energy is at a minimum in the equilibrium configuration, and thermal energy manifests primarily as vibrational kinetic energy.

Liquids: Particles remain close together (similar density to solids) but lack long-range order. They can slide past one another, enabling flow. Intermolecular forces are still significant but not strong enough to lock particles in fixed positions. Particles have both vibrational and translational kinetic energy.

Gases: Particles are widely separated (typically ~10 times the particle diameter). Intermolecular forces are negligible except during brief collisions. Particles move in straight lines between collisions, with kinetic energy dominating over potential energy. This explains why gases fill containers and are easily compressed.

Internal Energy

Internal energy (U) is the total energy stored within a system at the microscopic level. It comprises:

U = Ek,random + Ep,molecular

where Ek,random is the random kinetic energy of molecular motion (translation, rotation, vibration) and Ep,molecular is the potential energy due to intermolecular forces.

Key Insight: Temperature is directly proportional to the average random kinetic energy per molecule. For an ideal monatomic gas: ⟨Ek⟩ = (3/2)kBT, where kB = 1.38 × 10-23 J K-1 is Boltzmann's constant.

Important Distinction: Internal energy is NOT the same as temperature. Two systems at the same temperature can have vastly different internal energies if they have different masses or are in different phases. A cup of boiling water and a swimming pool at the same temperature have the same average molecular kinetic energy, but the pool has far more total internal energy.

Temperature and Absolute Zero

The Kelvin scale is defined such that zero Kelvin (absolute zero) corresponds to the state of minimum possible internal energy—when molecular kinetic energy approaches zero. The conversion is:

T(K) = T(°C) + 273.15

At absolute zero, quantum mechanical zero-point energy prevents particles from being completely motionless, but classical thermal motion ceases.

Pressure in Gases

Gas pressure arises from momentum transfer during molecular collisions with container walls. Each collision imparts a small impulse, and with ~1023 collisions per second, we observe steady macroscopic pressure. The ideal gas equation relates macroscopic variables:

PV = nRT = NkBT

where P is pressure (Pa), V is volume (m³), n is moles, R = 8.31 J mol-1 K-1 is the gas constant, N is the number of molecules, and T is absolute temperature (K).

This equation reveals that for fixed volume, pressure increases linearly with temperature—faster moving molecules collide more frequently and with greater momentum change.

Specific Heat Capacity

When thermal energy is added to a substance without changing its phase, the temperature rises. The relationship between energy input and temperature change is quantified by specific heat capacity.

Definition and Formula

Specific heat capacity (c) is the energy required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 K (or 1°C). The fundamental equation is:

Q = mcΔT

where:

  • Q is thermal energy transferred (J)
  • m is mass (kg)
  • c is specific heat capacity (J kg-1 K-1)
  • ΔT is temperature change (K or °C)

Physical Interpretation

Why do different substances have different specific heat capacities? Consider what happens when energy is added:

  1. Molecular degrees of freedom: Molecules can store energy in translational, rotational, and vibrational modes. Complex molecules have more ways to store energy, typically resulting in higher heat capacity.
  2. Intermolecular forces: Energy may be used to overcome attractive forces as well as increase kinetic energy, particularly in liquids.
  3. Mass per particle: Lighter molecules achieve higher velocities (and thus temperature increase) for a given energy input.

Water has an exceptionally high specific heat capacity (cwater = 4180 J kg-1 K-1) due to hydrogen bonding and molecular structure. This is why water is excellent for thermal regulation in organisms and climate systems.

Phase Changes and Latent Heat

During phase transitions (melting, freezing, boiling, condensing), thermal energy is added or removed without temperature change. This energy alters molecular arrangement and potential energy rather than kinetic energy.

Latent Heat Definition

Latent heat is the energy required per unit mass to change phase at constant temperature:

Q = mL

where L is specific latent heat (J kg-1).

Specific latent heat of fusion (Lf): energy to change from solid to liquid (or vice versa).
Specific latent heat of vaporization (Lv): energy to change from liquid to gas (or vice versa).

For water:

  • Lf = 3.34 × 105 J kg-1
  • Lv = 2.26 × 106 J kg-1

Physical Mechanism

When ice melts, energy breaks hydrogen bonds between water molecules in the crystal lattice. The molecules gain potential energy as they move from ordered solid positions to the more random liquid state, but their average kinetic energy (temperature) remains constant at 0°C until all ice has melted.

Vaporization requires much more energy than fusion because molecules must be separated to gas-phase distances, completely overcoming intermolecular attractions. This is why steam burns are so severe—condensing steam releases enormous energy.

Mechanisms of Heat Transfer: Conduction

Heat transfer occurs through three mechanisms: conduction, convection, and radiation. Each operates through different physical principles.

Conduction Mechanism

Conduction is energy transfer through matter without bulk movement of the material. In solids, it occurs through:

  1. Lattice vibrations (phonons): Vibrating atoms transfer energy to neighbors through their connecting bonds
  2. Free electron diffusion: In metals, mobile electrons carry energy from hot to cold regions, making metals excellent conductors

Thermal Conductivity and Heat Flow Rate

The rate of heat flow by conduction through a material is given by:

Q/t = kAΔT/d

where:

  • Q/t or dQ/dt is power (rate of heat transfer) in watts (W)
  • k is thermal conductivity (W m-1 K-1)
  • A is cross-sectional area (m²)
  • ΔT is temperature difference (K)
  • d or dx is thickness (m)

Mechanisms of Heat Transfer: Convection

Convection is heat transfer by bulk movement of fluids (liquids or gases). It combines conduction within the fluid with macroscopic fluid flow.

Types of Convection

Natural (free) convection: Fluid motion driven by buoyancy forces arising from density differences. Hot fluid expands, becomes less dense, and rises; cool fluid sinks. Examples: heating a room, ocean currents, atmospheric circulation.

Forced convection: Fluid motion driven by external means (fans, pumps). Examples: car radiators, forced-air heating, cooling fans in electronics.

Mechanisms of Heat Transfer: Radiation

Thermal radiation is electromagnetic wave emission by objects due to their temperature. Unlike conduction and convection, radiation requires no medium—it can transfer energy across vacuum.

Stefan-Boltzmann Law

The power radiated by a perfect emitter (black body) is:

P = σAT4

where:

  • P is power radiated (W)
  • σ = 5.67 × 10-8 W m-2 K-4 is the Stefan-Boltzmann constant
  • A is surface area (m²)
  • T is absolute temperature (K)

The T4 dependence means doubling absolute temperature increases radiation by a factor of 16!

Real Surfaces: Emissivity

Real objects are not perfect emitters. Emissivity (ε) quantifies how effectively a surface radiates compared to a black body:

P = εσAT4

where 0 ≤ ε ≤ 1:

  • Black body: ε = 1
  • Dull/black surfaces: ε ≈ 0.9-0.95
  • Shiny metal surfaces: ε ≈ 0.05-0.2

Key Definitions

Internal Energy (U)
The total energy stored within a system at the microscopic level, equal to the sum of random kinetic energies and intermolecular potential energies of all particles. Measured in joules (J).
Specific Heat Capacity (c)
The energy required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 K (or 1°C). Units: J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. An intensive property that depends only on the material, not the amount.
Specific Latent Heat (L)
The energy required per unit mass to change the phase of a substance at constant temperature. Specific latent heat of fusion (Lf) for solid-liquid transitions; specific latent heat of vaporization (Lv) for liquid-gas transitions. Units: J kg⁻¹.
Thermal Conductivity (k)
A material property quantifying how effectively it conducts heat. Higher values indicate better conduction. Units: W m⁻¹ K⁻¹. Metals have high k; insulators have low k.
Conduction
Heat transfer through matter without bulk movement of the material, occurring via molecular collisions and lattice vibrations (phonons) in solids, or free electron diffusion in metals.
Convection
Heat transfer by bulk movement of fluids (liquids or gases) driven by buoyancy forces (natural convection) or external mechanisms like pumps or fans (forced convection).
Thermal Radiation
Heat transfer by electromagnetic waves emitted by all objects due to their temperature. Requires no medium and can occur across vacuum. Governed by the Stefan-Boltzmann law.
Emissivity (ε)
A dimensionless factor (0 ≤ ε ≤ 1) describing how effectively a surface radiates thermal energy compared to an ideal black body (ε = 1). Good absorbers have high emissivity; shiny reflective surfaces have low emissivity.

Practice Problems

Problem 1 A 0.80 kg copper block (specific heat capacity 385 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹) at 120°C is placed in 1.5 kg of water at 15°C in an insulated container. Calculate the final equilibrium temperature. ▼
Solution

Using conservation of energy: energy lost by copper = energy gained by water

mccc(Tc - Tf) = mwcw(Tf - Tw)

0.80 × 385 × (120 - Tf) = 1.5 × 4180 × (Tf - 15)

308(120 - Tf) = 6270(Tf - 15)

36,960 - 308Tf = 6270Tf - 94,050

131,010 = 6578Tf

Tf = 19.9°C ≈ 20°C

Explanation

Energy flows from the hot copper to the cold water until thermal equilibrium is reached. The final temperature is much closer to the initial water temperature because water has both greater mass AND greater specific heat capacity, giving it much larger thermal inertia. The copper's relatively small heat capacity means it cools substantially while causing only a small temperature rise in the water.

Problem 2 An ice cube of mass 45 g at 0°C is placed in 200 g of water at 25°C. Does all the ice melt? What is the final temperature? ▼
Solution

(a) Energy required to melt all ice:
Q₁ = mLf = 0.045 × 3.34 × 10⁵ = 15,030 J

Energy available from water cooling to 0°C:
Q₂ = mcΔT = 0.200 × 4180 × 25 = 20,900 J

Since Q₂ > Q₁, sufficient energy exists to melt all the ice.

(b) After melting, energy remaining:
Qremaining = 20,900 - 15,030 = 5,870 J

This heats the melted ice and original water:
Qremaining = (mice + mwater)cΔT
5,870 = (0.045 + 0.200) × 4180 × ΔT
ΔT = 5,870 / (0.245 × 4180) = 5.73 K

Final temperature: Tf = 0 + 5.73 = 5.7°C

Explanation

This problem requires checking whether complete phase change occurs before calculating final temperature. We first determine if enough energy exists to complete the phase change. Since it does, we use the remaining energy to find the temperature rise. If insufficient energy existed, some ice would remain at 0°C, and the final temperature would be 0°C with a mixture of ice and water.

Problem 3 A double-glazed window consists of two 4.0 mm glass panes (k = 0.80 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹) separated by 12 mm air gap (k = 0.024 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹). Window area 1.2 m². Temperature difference 18 K. Calculate thermal resistance and heat flow rate. ▼
Solution

(a) Thermal resistances in series add:

R₁ = d₁/(k₁A) = 0.004/(0.80 × 1.2) = 0.00417 K W⁻¹

Rair = dair/(kairA) = 0.012/(0.024 × 1.2) = 0.417 K W⁻¹

R₂ = d₂/(k₂A) = 0.004/(0.80 × 1.2) = 0.00417 K W⁻¹

Rtotal = R₁ + Rair + R₂ = 0.00417 + 0.417 + 0.00417 = 0.425 K W⁻¹

(b) Heat flow rate:
Q/t = ΔT/Rtotal = 18/0.425 = 42.4 W

Explanation

The air gap dominates the thermal resistance (98% of total) despite being three times thicker than the combined glass thickness. This demonstrates why air is an excellent insulator when trapped (preventing convection). A single 8 mm glass pane would have R = 0.00833 K W⁻¹, giving Q/t = 2,590 W—about 61 times greater heat loss! The double-glazing with air gap dramatically improves insulation.

Problem 4 The Sun has radius 6.96 × 10⁸ m and surface temperature 5800 K. Assuming black body emission, calculate total power radiated and intensity at Earth's distance (1.50 × 10¹¹ m). ▼
Solution

(a) Surface area of Sun:
A = 4πr² = 4π(6.96 × 10⁸)² = 6.09 × 10¹⁸ m²

Power radiated:
P = σAT⁴ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ × 6.09 × 10¹⁸ × (5800)⁴
= 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ × 6.09 × 10¹⁸ × 1.13 × 10¹⁵
= 3.90 × 10²⁶ W

(b) This power spreads over sphere of radius rEarth:
Area at Earth = 4πr²Earth = 4π(1.50 × 10¹¹)² = 2.83 × 10²³ m²

Intensity at Earth:
I = P/AEarth = 3.90 × 10²⁶ / 2.83 × 10²³ = 1380 W m⁻²

Explanation

The Sun radiates enormous power (equivalent to ~4 × 10⁹ billion 1 GW power plants!). By the time this radiation reaches Earth, it's spread over an enormous spherical area, reducing intensity to ~1380 W m⁻² (the solar constant). This inverse-square law dilution is why solar radiation intensity decreases with distance.

Real-World Applications

Climate and Ocean Thermal Inertia

Water's high specific heat capacity makes oceans massive thermal reservoirs. They absorb enormous energy with small temperature changes, moderating coastal climates. This is why coastal regions have smaller temperature variations than inland areas.

Building Insulation

Effective insulation uses materials with low thermal conductivity (fiberglass, foam) and traps air, which has very low conductivity. Double-glazed windows reduce conduction through glass and convection in the air gap. Low-emissivity coatings reduce radiative heat transfer.

Thermal Management in Electronics

Modern processors dissipate 100+ watts in small areas. Heat is conducted to metal heat sinks, which have large surface areas and fins to maximize convection. Fans force air flow for enhanced convection. High-performance systems use liquid cooling for greater heat capacity and thermal conductivity.

Space Exploration

In space's vacuum, convection is impossible. Spacecraft use radiators to emit thermal radiation into space. Thermal blankets with low emissivity minimize radiation loss. The James Webb Space Telescope uses a multi-layer sunshield to maintain instruments at ~40 K while the sunlit side reaches ~380 K.

Common Misconceptions

Misconception 1: Heat and Temperature Are the Same

Reality: Heat (thermal energy, Q) is energy transferred between systems. Temperature is a measure of average molecular kinetic energy. A large cold object can contain more thermal energy than a small hot object.

Misconception 2: Cold Is a Substance That Flows

Reality: There is no "cold" substance. Heat flows from hot to cold regions. When you touch something cold, heat flows OUT of your hand, not "cold" INTO your hand.

Misconception 3: Metals Are Colder Than Wood at Room Temperature

Reality: At thermal equilibrium, they're the same temperature. Metals feel colder because high thermal conductivity means they conduct heat away from your hand rapidly, while wood's low conductivity means slower heat loss.

Misconception 4: Boiling Water Is Always at 100°C

Reality: 100°C is water's boiling point at standard atmospheric pressure (101.3 kPa). At higher altitudes (lower pressure), water boils at lower temperatures. At higher pressures (pressure cookers), it boils above 100°C.

Exam Tips

Always convert temperatures to Kelvin when using the Stefan-Boltzmann law. For specific heat capacity, Kelvin and Celsius give the same ΔT.
When solving thermal equilibrium problems, set up energy conservation carefully: energy LOST by hot objects equals energy GAINED by cold objects.
In multi-stage problems, work systematically through each stage. Draw a heating curve sketch to identify all stages.
State assumptions explicitly when solving problems, such as "Assuming no heat loss to surroundings."
Distinguish clearly between heat (energy transfer), internal energy (total microscopic energy), and temperature (average kinetic energy).
