Key Definitions

Lewis Acid
An electron pair acceptor. A species with an empty orbital that can accept a lone pair of electrons to form a coordinate (dative covalent) bond.
Lewis Base
An electron pair donor. A species with at least one lone pair of electrons that can be donated to form a coordinate bond.
Buffer Solution
A solution that resists changes in pH when small amounts of acid or base are added. Consists of a weak acid and its conjugate base (or weak base and its conjugate acid) in appreciable concentrations.
Equivalence Point
The point in a titration where the number of moles of acid equals the number of moles of base (for monoprotic species). The stoichiometric point of complete neutralization.
Half-Equivalence Point
The point in a weak acid or weak base titration where exactly half of the acid (or base) has been neutralized. At this point, pH = pKa (or pOH = pKb).
pKa
The negative logarithm of the acid dissociation constant: pKa = -log(Ka). A measure of acid strength; lower pKa indicates a stronger acid.
Polyprotic Acid
An acid capable of donating more than one proton per molecule. Each proton has its own Ka value (Ka1, Ka2, etc.), with Ka1 > Ka2 > Ka3.

18.1 Lewis Acids and Bases

While the Brønsted-Lowry definition (Topic 8) focuses on proton transfer, the Lewis definition provides a broader framework that encompasses reactions that don't involve protons at all.

The Lewis Definition: Electron Pair Transfer

Gilbert N. Lewis proposed a more general definition in 1923:

  • Lewis acid: electron pair acceptor (electrophile)
  • Lewis base: electron pair donor (nucleophile)

This definition is more general because ALL Brønsted-Lowry acids and bases are also Lewis acids and bases, but NOT all Lewis acids and bases are Brønsted-Lowry species.

Why Lewis Acids Accept Electron Pairs

Lewis acids typically have:

  1. An incomplete octet (e.g., BF₃, AlCl₃)
  2. A vacant orbital available for bonding
  3. High positive charge density (metal cations like Fe³⁺, Cu²⁺)

Example: BF₃ and NH₃

Boron trifluoride (BF₃) is a classic Lewis acid:

  • Boron has only 6 electrons in its valence shell (incomplete octet)
  • The empty p orbital can accept an electron pair
  • Ammonia (NH₃) has a lone pair on nitrogen

Reaction: BF₃ + NH₃ → F₃B←NH₃

The arrow (←) represents a coordinate (dative covalent) bond where both electrons come from the nitrogen atom. After formation, this bond is indistinguishable from other B-N bonds.

Metal Cations as Lewis Acids

Transition metal ions form complex ions by accepting electron pairs from ligands:

[Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺: The Fe³⁺ ion (Lewis acid) accepts six lone pairs from water molecules (Lewis bases)

The high charge density of metal cations creates a strong attraction for electron pairs. This explains:

  • Complex ion formation
  • Coordination chemistry
  • Catalytic activity of metal ions

Hydration of Metal Ions

When metal salts dissolve in water, the metal cations undergo Lewis acid-base reactions:

Al³⁺(aq) + 6H₂O(l) → [Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺(aq)

Here, Al³⁺ is the Lewis acid and H₂O is the Lewis base. The coordinated water molecules become more acidic due to the polarizing effect of the metal ion:

[Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ [Al(H₂O)₅OH]²⁺(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq)

This explains why solutions of Al³⁺, Fe³⁺, and other highly charged cations are acidic.

Common Lewis Acid-Base Reactions

  1. Adduct formation: CO + BH₃ → H₃B←CO (both C and O have lone pairs; CO donates through C)
  2. Ligand substitution: [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 4NH₃ → [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ + 4H₂O
  3. Friedel-Crafts reactions: AlCl₃ acts as a Lewis acid catalyst in organic chemistry

Distinguishing Brønsted-Lowry and Lewis

All Brønsted-Lowry reactions are Lewis reactions:

  • HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻
  • Brønsted: HCl donates H⁺, H₂O accepts H⁺
  • Lewis: H⁺ (Lewis acid) accepts electron pair from H₂O (Lewis base)

But some Lewis reactions involve no protons:

  • BF₃ + F⁻ → BF₄⁻ (purely Lewis acid-base)
  • Ag⁺ + 2NH₃ → [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ (complex ion formation)

18.2 Calculations Involving Acids and Bases

This section extends Topic 8 calculations to more complex scenarios involving weak acids, weak bases, and buffer solutions.

Review: Ka and Kb Expressions

For a weak acid HA:
HA(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + A⁻(aq)
Ka = [H₃O⁺][A⁻] / [HA]

For a weak base B:
B(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ BH⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)
Kb = [BH⁺][OH⁻] / [B]

The Ka × Kb Relationship

For any conjugate acid-base pair at 25°C:

Ka × Kb = Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴

This relationship is derived from adding the ionization reactions:
HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻ (Ka)
A⁻ + H₂O ⇌ HA + OH⁻ (Kb)
Net: H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻ (Kw)

Therefore: pKa + pKb = pKw = 14.00 (at 25°C)

Buffer Solutions: The Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation

A buffer resists pH change because it contains both:

  1. An acid (to neutralize added base)
  2. A base (to neutralize added acid)

For an acidic buffer (weak acid + conjugate base):
pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA])

For a basic buffer (weak base + conjugate acid):
pOH = pKb + log([BH⁺]/[B])

Why Buffers Work

Consider an acetic acid/acetate buffer:

  • CH₃COOH (weak acid) present in large amount
  • CH₃COO⁻ (conjugate base) present in large amount

When small amount of H⁺ is added:
CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺ → CH₃COOH (base consumes the acid)

When small amount of OH⁻ is added:
CH₃COOH + OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O (acid consumes the base)

In both cases, the ratio [A⁻]/[HA] changes only slightly, so pH changes only slightly.

Buffer Capacity and Range

Buffer capacity: the amount of acid or base a buffer can absorb before significant pH change occurs. Maximum when [A⁻] = [HA], i.e., when pH = pKa.

Buffer range: typically pH = pKa ± 1. Outside this range, the buffer becomes ineffective because one component becomes negligible.

Polyprotic Acids

Polyprotic acids have multiple ionizable protons, each with its own Ka:

H₃PO₄ ⇌ H⁺ + H₂PO₄⁻ (Ka1 = 7.5 × 10⁻³, pKa1 = 2.12)
H₂PO₄⁻ ⇌ H⁺ + HPO₄²⁻ (Ka2 = 6.2 × 10⁻⁸, pKa2 = 7.21)
HPO₄²⁻ ⇌ H⁺ + PO₄³⁻ (Ka3 = 4.8 × 10⁻¹³, pKa3 = 12.32)

Key principle: Ka1 >> Ka2 >> Ka3

Why? After losing the first proton, the remaining ion is negatively charged, making it harder to remove the next proton.

18.3 pH Curves and Titrations

pH curves (titration curves) show how pH changes as a base is added to an acid (or vice versa). The shape depends on the strength of the acid and base.

Four Types of Acid-Base Titrations

  1. Strong acid + Strong base (e.g., HCl + NaOH)
  2. Weak acid + Strong base (e.g., CH₃COOH + NaOH)
  3. Strong acid + Weak base (e.g., HCl + NH₃)
  4. Weak acid + Weak base (not commonly used; no sharp endpoint)

Strong Acid + Strong Base Titration

Key features:

  • Initial pH: Low (strong acid, e.g., pH ≈ 1 for 0.1 M HCl)
  • Buffer region: None (no weak acid/conjugate base pair)
  • Equivalence point: pH = 7 (neutral, only Na⁺ and Cl⁻ present)
  • Sharp rise: Very steep around equivalence point (pH jumps from ~3 to ~11)
  • Post-equivalence: Excess strong base determines pH

Weak Acid + Strong Base Titration

Key features:

  • Initial pH: Higher than strong acid (e.g., pH ≈ 2.9 for 0.1 M CH₃COOH)
  • Buffer region: Significant region before equivalence where pH changes gradually
  • Half-equivalence point: pH = pKa (exactly half the acid neutralized; [A⁻] = [HA])
  • Equivalence point: pH > 7 (basic, due to conjugate base hydrolysis)
  • Less sharp rise: Still significant but not as steep as strong-strong

Strong Acid + Weak Base Titration

Key features:

  • Initial pH: Higher than weak acid (weak base in water, pH ≈ 11 for 0.1 M NH₃)
  • Buffer region: Present, where NH₃ and NH₄⁺ coexist
  • Half-equivalence point: pOH = pKb (or pH = pKa of NH₄⁺)
  • Equivalence point: pH < 7 (acidic, due to conjugate acid hydrolysis)
  • Inverted shape: pH decreases as acid is added

Indicator Selection

An indicator is a weak acid (HIn) with different colored forms:
HIn ⇌ H⁺ + In⁻
(color A) (color B)

Indicator changes color over a pH range of approximately pKa(indicator) ± 1.

Choosing the right indicator:

  • The indicator's transition range must include the pH at the equivalence point
  • The steeper the rise, the more indicators will work

Examples:

  • Phenolphthalein (pH 8.2-10.0, colorless to pink): Good for weak acid + strong base
  • Methyl orange (pH 3.1-4.4, red to yellow): Good for strong acid + weak base
  • Bromothymol blue (pH 6.0-7.6, yellow to blue): Good for strong acid + strong base

Polyprotic Acid Titrations

Polyprotic acids show multiple equivalence points:

H₂SO₃ + NaOH titration:

  • First equivalence point: H₂SO₃ → HSO₃⁻
  • Second equivalence point: HSO₃⁻ → SO₃²⁻

Each equivalence point appears as a separate inflection on the pH curve. If the Ka values are sufficiently different (Ka1/Ka2 > 10⁴), the two endpoints are clearly separated.

Real-World Applications

Blood pH Buffering

Human blood maintains pH 7.35-7.45 using the carbonic acid/bicarbonate buffer:

H₂CO₃(aq) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + HCO₃⁻(aq) (pKa = 6.1)

Though pKa = 6.1 is not optimal for pH 7.4, this system works because:

  1. CO₂ levels can be adjusted by breathing rate
  2. Kidneys can excrete or retain HCO₃⁻
  3. Large concentrations of both components

Acidosis (pH < 7.35): excess H⁺, caused by diabetes, kidney disease, or respiratory problems
Alkalosis (pH > 7.45): deficiency of H⁺, caused by hyperventilation or vomiting

Ocean Acidification

CO₂ from the atmosphere dissolves in seawater:

CO₂(g) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₂CO₃(aq) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + HCO₃⁻(aq)

Increased atmospheric CO₂ shifts equilibrium right, increasing [H⁺] and decreasing pH. Ocean pH has dropped from 8.2 to 8.1 since the industrial revolution (a 26% increase in [H⁺]!).

Industrial pH Control

Fermentation: yeast produces acids during beer/wine making; buffers maintain optimal pH

Pharmaceuticals: drug stability and bioavailability often depend on pH; buffers in formulations

Wastewater treatment: buffers maintain optimal pH for bacterial decomposition

Catalysis

Lewis acids are essential catalysts:

  • AlCl₃ in Friedel-Crafts alkylation/acylation
  • BF₃ in polymerization reactions
  • Transition metal complexes in asymmetric catalysis

Practice Problems

Problem 1: Identifying Lewis Acids and Bases ▼
Question: Identify the Lewis acid and Lewis base in each reaction:
(a) Ag⁺ + 2CN⁻ → [Ag(CN)₂]⁻
(b) BF₃ + NH₃ → F₃B-NH₃
(c) AlCl₃ + Cl⁻ → AlCl₄⁻
Solution:
(a) Lewis acid: Ag⁺ (accepts electron pairs from CN⁻); Lewis base: CN⁻ (donates electron pairs)
(b) Lewis acid: BF₃ (accepts electron pair from N); Lewis base: NH₃ (donates lone pair from N)
(c) Lewis acid: AlCl₃ (accepts electron pair from Cl⁻); Lewis base: Cl⁻ (donates electron pair)
Explanation: Lewis acids have vacant orbitals or are electron-deficient. Metal cations (Ag⁺) and molecules with incomplete octets (BF₃, AlCl₃) readily accept electron pairs. Lewis bases have lone pairs available for donation (CN⁻, NH₃, Cl⁻).
Problem 2: Buffer pH Calculation ▼
Question: Calculate the pH of a buffer solution containing 0.250 M NH₃ and 0.150 M NH₄Cl. Kb for NH₃ = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵.
Solution:
First, find pKb: pKb = -log(1.8 × 10⁻⁵) = 4.74

For a basic buffer: pOH = pKb + log([NH₄⁺]/[NH₃])
pOH = 4.74 + log(0.150/0.250)
pOH = 4.74 + log(0.60)
pOH = 4.74 + (-0.22)
pOH = 4.52

pH = 14.00 - 4.52 = 9.48
Explanation: For basic buffers, use the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation in the form with pOH and pKb. The ratio [conjugate acid]/[base] determines how far from pKb the pOH will be. Since [NH₄⁺] < [NH₃], pOH < pKb, making the solution more basic (higher pH) than if concentrations were equal.
Problem 3: Weak Acid Titration ▼
Question: A 25.0 mL sample of 0.100 M propanoic acid (CH₃CH₂COOH, Ka = 1.3 × 10⁻⁵) is titrated with 0.100 M NaOH. Calculate:
(a) The initial pH
(b) The pH at the half-equivalence point
(c) The pH at the equivalence point
Solution:
(a) For weak acid: Ka = [H⁺]²/[HA], so [H⁺] = √(Ka × [HA]) = √(1.3 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.100) = 1.14 × 10⁻³ M
pH = -log(1.14 × 10⁻³) = 2.94

(b) At half-equivalence: pH = pKa = -log(1.3 × 10⁻⁵) = 4.89

(c) At equivalence: moles of propanoate = 0.100 × 0.025 = 0.00250 mol in 50.0 mL
[C₂H₅COO⁻] = 0.00250/0.050 = 0.0500 M
Kb = Kw/Ka = (1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴)/(1.3 × 10⁻⁵) = 7.7 × 10⁻¹⁰
[OH⁻] = √(Kb × [A⁻]) = √(7.7 × 10⁻¹⁰ × 0.0500) = 6.2 × 10⁻⁶ M
pOH = 5.21, pH = 8.79
Explanation: Part (a): Weak acids undergo partial ionization; use the approximation when Ka is small. Part (b): At half-equivalence, [A⁻] = [HA], so the log term in Henderson-Hasselbalch equals zero. Part (c): The conjugate base hydrolyzes, making the solution basic. This is why weak acid-strong base titrations have basic equivalence points.
Problem 4: Buffer with Added Acid/Base ▼
Question: A buffer is prepared with 0.50 mol CH₃COOH and 0.50 mol CH₃COONa in 1.0 L of solution (Ka = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵). Calculate the pH after adding:
(a) 0.10 mol HCl
(b) 0.10 mol NaOH
Solution:
Initial pH: pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]) = 4.74 + log(0.50/0.50) = 4.74

(a) HCl reacts with CH₃COO⁻: CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺ → CH₃COOH
After: [CH₃COO⁻] = 0.50 - 0.10 = 0.40 M; [CH₃COOH] = 0.50 + 0.10 = 0.60 M
pH = 4.74 + log(0.40/0.60) = 4.74 + (-0.18) = 4.56

(b) NaOH reacts with CH₃COOH: CH₃COOH + OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O
After: [CH₃COOH] = 0.50 - 0.10 = 0.40 M; [CH₃COO⁻] = 0.50 + 0.10 = 0.60 M
pH = 4.74 + log(0.60/0.40) = 4.74 + 0.18 = 4.92
Explanation: The buffer responds by converting one component to the other. Adding acid increases [HA] and decreases [A⁻], shifting pH below pKa. Adding base does the opposite. The pH change is small (±0.18 units) because both components remain in similar concentrations. Without the buffer, 0.10 M HCl would give pH 1, and 0.10 M NaOH would give pH 13!
Problem 5: Indicator Selection ▼
Question: Which indicator would be most appropriate for titrating 0.10 M HNO₃ with 0.10 M KOH? Choose from:
(a) Methyl orange (pH 3.1-4.4)
(b) Bromothymol blue (pH 6.0-7.6)
(c) Phenolphthalein (pH 8.2-10.0)
Justify your answer.
Solution: Answer: (b) Bromothymol blue

Justification: This is a strong acid + strong base titration, so the equivalence point occurs at pH 7. The indicator's transition range must include pH 7. Bromothymol blue (6.0-7.6) is centered on pH 7, making it ideal. Methyl orange would change too early, and phenolphthalein would change too late, though phenolphthalein could work due to the steep pH curve around equivalence.
Explanation: For strong-strong titrations, the pH curve is very steep near equivalence (pH jumps from ~4 to ~10), so many indicators work. However, the best choice has a transition range centered on the equivalence point pH. For weak acid-strong base, use an indicator transitioning in the basic range (phenolphthalein). For strong acid-weak base, use one transitioning in the acidic range (methyl orange).
Problem 6: Polyprotic Acid ▼
Question: Phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄) has three dissociation constants:
Ka1 = 7.5 × 10⁻³, Ka2 = 6.2 × 10⁻⁸, Ka3 = 4.8 × 10⁻¹³
Calculate the pH of a 0.10 M H₃PO₄ solution.
Solution:
Since Ka1 >> Ka2 >> Ka3, the pH is determined almost entirely by the first ionization:
H₃PO₄ ⇌ H⁺ + H₂PO₄⁻
Ka1 = [H⁺][H₂PO₄⁻]/[H₃PO₄] = 7.5 × 10⁻³

Let x = [H⁺] at equilibrium
7.5 × 10⁻³ = x²/(0.10 - x)

Since Ka1 is relatively large, we cannot assume x << 0.10. Using the quadratic formula:
x² + 0.0075x - 0.00075 = 0
x = [-0.0075 + √(0.0075² + 4(0.00075))]/2 = 0.0234 M

pH = -log(0.0234) = 1.63
Explanation: For polyprotic acids, if Ka1/Ka2 > 1000, the second and third ionizations contribute negligibly to [H⁺]. Here, Ka1/Ka2 ≈ 120,000. However, because Ka1 is not very small compared to the concentration, we must use the quadratic equation rather than the simplifying approximation.
Problem 7: Conjugate Acid-Base Pair ▼
Question: A weak acid HA has Ka = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵. Calculate:
(a) The Kb of its conjugate base A⁻
(b) The pH of a 0.20 M solution of NaA
Solution:
(a) Ka × Kb = Kw
Kb = Kw/Ka = (1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴)/(1.0 × 10⁻⁵) = 1.0 × 10⁻⁹

(b) A⁻ + H₂O ⇌ HA + OH⁻
Kb = [HA][OH⁻]/[A⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻⁹
Let x = [OH⁻]
1.0 × 10⁻⁹ = x²/0.20
x² = 2.0 × 10⁻¹⁰
x = [OH⁻] = 1.41 × 10⁻⁵ M

pOH = -log(1.41 × 10⁻⁵) = 4.85
pH = 14.00 - 4.85 = 9.15
Explanation: The conjugate base of a weak acid is itself a weak base. The weaker the acid (larger pKa), the stronger its conjugate base (smaller pKb). A salt of a weak acid (NaA) produces a basic solution because A⁻ hydrolyzes. This explains why the equivalence point in weak acid-strong base titrations is basic.
Problem 8: Lewis Acid Strength ▼
Question: Explain why [Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺ solutions are acidic, while [Na(H₂O)₆]⁺ solutions are neutral. Use Lewis acid-base theory in your answer.
Solution:
Al³⁺ is a small, highly charged cation (charge density = 3+/ionic radius). This creates strong polarization of the coordinated water molecules, making the O-H bonds more polar and easier to break:

[Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺ + H₂O ⇌ [Al(H₂O)₅OH]²⁺ + H₃O⁺

The Al³⁺ acts as a Lewis acid, withdrawing electron density from the water ligands and making them more acidic. The resulting solution has pH ≈ 3.

Na⁺ is a large cation with only 1+ charge, so its charge density is much lower. It polarizes coordinated water molecules only weakly, so they remain essentially neutral. [Na(H₂O)₆]⁺ does not significantly protonate water, giving pH ≈ 7.
Explanation: This demonstrates that Lewis acidity (electron pair acceptance) can indirectly create Brønsted acidity (proton donation). Highly charged metal cations (Al³⁺, Fe³⁺, Cr³⁺) give acidic solutions, while Group 1 and 2 cations (except Be²⁺) give neutral solutions. This is important in understanding the pH of salt solutions and the behavior of metal ions in biological systems.
Problem 9: Complete Titration Calculation ▼
Question: A 50.0 mL sample of 0.200 M benzoic acid (C₆H₅COOH, Ka = 6.3 × 10⁻⁵) is titrated with 0.100 M NaOH.
(a) What volume of NaOH is required to reach the equivalence point?
(b) Calculate the pH at the equivalence point.
(c) Would bromothymol blue (pH 6.0-7.6) be a suitable indicator? Explain.
Solution:
(a) Moles of C₆H₅COOH = 0.200 × 0.050 = 0.0100 mol
At equivalence, moles NaOH = moles acid = 0.0100 mol
Volume NaOH = 0.0100/0.100 = 0.100 L = 100 mL

(b) Total volume = 50.0 + 100 = 150 mL = 0.150 L
[C₆H₅COO⁻] = 0.0100/0.150 = 0.0667 M
Kb = Kw/Ka = (1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴)/(6.3 × 10⁻⁵) = 1.59 × 10⁻¹⁰
[OH⁻] = √(Kb × [A⁻]) = √(1.59 × 10⁻¹⁰ × 0.0667) = 3.26 × 10⁻⁶ M
pOH = 5.49, pH = 8.51

(c) No, bromothymol blue is not suitable. Its transition range (6.0-7.6) does not include the equivalence point pH of 8.51. Phenolphthalein (8.2-10.0) would be a better choice as its range includes pH 8.51.
Explanation: This is a weak acid-strong base titration, so the equivalence point is basic (pH > 7) due to hydrolysis of the conjugate base. The volume calculation uses stoichiometry (1:1 mole ratio for monoprotic acid). The pH calculation treats the equivalence point solution as a solution of weak base. Indicator selection requires matching the transition range to the equivalence point pH, not to pH 7.

0.02.87

Using this data:
(a) Identify the type of titration

(b) Equivalence point volume = 50.0 mL (where pH jumps sharply from 6.27 to 8.72)

(c) Half-equivalence point is at 25.0 mL (half of 50.0 mL), where pH = pKa.

Exam Tips

  • At half-equivalence in a weak acid-strong base titration, pH = pKa. This is the easiest way to determine pKa from a titration curve.
  • Buffer problems: always write the neutralization reaction first, calculate new amounts, then use Henderson-Hasselbalch. Don't skip the stoichiometry step!
  • For equivalence point pH calculations, remember: weak acid + strong base gives basic pH (conjugate base hydrolyzes); strong acid + weak base gives acidic pH (conjugate acid hydrolyzes).
  • When choosing indicators, match the indicator's transition range to the equivalence point pH, not to pH 7. Only strong-strong titrations have equivalence at pH 7.
  • Lewis acids must have an empty orbital or be able to accept electrons. Common examples: BF₃ (incomplete octet), metal cations (vacant d orbitals), H⁺ (vacant 1s).
  • In polyprotic acid calculations, if Ka1/Ka2 > 1000, ignore the second ionization for pH calculations. The first ionization dominates.
  • Buffer capacity is maximum when pH = pKa (i.e., when [A⁻] = [HA]). Buffers work in the range pKa ± 1.
  • For conjugate acid-base pairs, Ka × Kb = Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25°C. This means pKa + pKb = 14.00. Use this to convert between Ka and Kb.
  • On pH curves, look for: (1) Initial pH tells acid/base strength, (2) Buffer region indicates weak acid or base, (3) Equivalence point pH tells what type of titration, (4) Steep region indicates good endpoint detection.
  • Common exam error: confusing equivalence point (stoichiometric point) with endpoint (where indicator changes). They're different concepts, though we choose indicators to make them coincide.
  • For Lewis acid-base reactions, draw electron arrows showing electron pair movement from base to acid. Remember both electrons in the coordinate bond come from the base.
  • In buffer calculations after adding acid/base, volumes cancel in the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation if both components are in the same solution. Use moles directly: pH = pKa + log(moles A⁻/moles HA).
  • Highly charged small cations (Al³⁺, Fe³⁺) make their aqueous solutions acidic by polarizing coordinated water molecules. Large, singly charged cations (Na⁺, K⁺) do not.
  • When a question asks "explain using equations," always write the relevant chemical equation. For buffer action, show the neutralization reaction. For equivalence point pH, show the hydrolysis reaction.
