Key Definitions

Dynamic Equilibrium
A state in a reversible reaction where the forward and reverse reactions occur at equal rates, resulting in no net change in the concentrations of reactants and products. The system appears static macroscopically but is dynamic at the molecular level.
Equilibrium Constant (Kc)
A dimensionless ratio of product concentrations to reactant concentrations at equilibrium, each raised to the power of their stoichiometric coefficients. For aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD: Kc = [C]^c[D]^d / [A]^a[B]^b
Equilibrium Constant (Kp)
The equilibrium constant expressed in terms of partial pressures for gaseous reactions. Kp has the same form as Kc but uses partial pressures instead of concentrations.
Le Chatelier's Principle
When a system at equilibrium is subjected to a change in concentration, temperature, or pressure, the system shifts to counteract the change and re-establish equilibrium.
Reaction Quotient (Q)
A ratio with the same form as K but calculated using current concentrations (not necessarily at equilibrium). Comparing Q to K determines the direction of reaction shift.
Position of Equilibrium
The relative amounts of reactants and products present at equilibrium. When K >> 1, equilibrium lies to the right (products favored); when K << 1, equilibrium lies to the left (reactants favored).
Homogeneous Equilibrium
An equilibrium in which all reactants and products are in the same phase (e.g., all gases or all in solution).
Heterogeneous Equilibrium
An equilibrium involving species in different phases. Pure solids and pure liquids are omitted from the equilibrium expression.

Introduction to Chemical Equilibrium

Chemical equilibrium is one of the most fundamental concepts in chemistry, bridging thermodynamics and kinetics. Many reactions we encounter are reversible—they can proceed in both forward and reverse directions. Understanding equilibrium is essential for predicting reaction outcomes, optimizing industrial processes, and explaining biological systems.

Prerequisites: Before diving into equilibrium, you should be comfortable with:

  • Concentration calculations and molarity (Topic 1)
  • Rate laws and reaction kinetics (Topic 6)
  • Basic thermodynamics and enthalpy changes (Topics 5 and 15)
  • Stoichiometry and balanced equations (Topic 1)

What Makes a Reaction Reversible?

Most reactions we write with a single arrow (→) appear to go to completion, but in reality, almost all reactions are reversible to some degree. A reversible reaction is indicated by a double arrow (⇌). Consider the synthesis of ammonia:

$$\text{N}_2(g) + 3\text{H}_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\text{NH}_3(g)$$

When nitrogen and hydrogen are mixed, they react to form ammonia (forward reaction). Simultaneously, ammonia molecules decompose back into nitrogen and hydrogen (reverse reaction). Initially, when we start with only reactants, the forward rate is high and the reverse rate is zero. As products form, the forward rate decreases (less reactant available) and the reverse rate increases (more product available).

Dynamic Equilibrium Established

Eventually, the forward and reverse rates become equal. This is dynamic equilibrium. The word 'dynamic' is crucial—reactions haven't stopped; both processes continue at equal rates. The concentrations remain constant, but molecules are continuously converting between reactants and products.

Key characteristics of dynamic equilibrium:

  1. Forward and reverse rates are equal
  2. Concentrations of all species remain constant (but not necessarily equal)
  3. The system must be closed (no exchange of matter with surroundings)
  4. Equilibrium can be approached from either direction (starting with all reactants, all products, or any mixture)
  5. Macroscopic properties (color, pressure, pH) remain constant

The Equilibrium Law and Equilibrium Constants

Deriving the Equilibrium Expression

For any reversible reaction at a given temperature:

$$a\text{A} + b\text{B} \rightleftharpoons c\text{C} + d\text{D}$$

The equilibrium law states that the ratio of products to reactants at equilibrium is constant:

$$K_c = \frac{[\text{C}]^c[\text{D}]^d}{[\text{A}]^a[\text{B}]^b}$$

Where:

  • Square brackets [ ] denote concentration in mol dm⁻³
  • Superscripts are the stoichiometric coefficients
  • Kc is the equilibrium constant for concentrations
  • Kc is dimensionless (technically) though units are often shown
  • Kc depends only on temperature

Important Rules for Writing K Expressions:

  1. Products always on top, reactants on bottom
  2. Pure solids and pure liquids are omitted (their concentrations don't change)
  3. Water is included only if it's not the solvent (in aqueous solutions, omit water unless it's a reactant/product in limited amount)
  4. Each concentration is raised to its stoichiometric coefficient

Example 1: Writing Equilibrium Expressions

For the reaction: $2\text{SO}_2(g) + \text{O}_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\text{SO}_3(g)$

$$K_c = \frac{[\text{SO}_3]^2}{[\text{SO}_2]^2[\text{O}_2]}$$

For the heterogeneous equilibrium: $\text{CaCO}_3(s) \rightleftharpoons \text{CaO}(s) + \text{CO}_2(g)$

$$K_c = [\text{CO}_2]$$

(The solids are omitted because their concentrations are constant and incorporated into K)

Interpreting the Magnitude of K

  • K >> 1 (e.g., K = 10⁶): Equilibrium lies far to the right; reaction proceeds almost to completion; products heavily favored
  • K ≈ 1 (e.g., K = 0.1 to 10): Significant amounts of both reactants and products at equilibrium
  • K << 1 (e.g., K = 10⁻⁶): Equilibrium lies far to the left; very little product forms; reactants favored

Common Misconception: A large K does NOT mean the reaction is fast. K tells us about the equilibrium position (thermodynamics), not the rate (kinetics). A reaction with K = 10¹⁰ might take years to reach equilibrium without a catalyst.

Equilibrium Constants for Gaseous Systems: Kp

For reactions involving gases, we can express the equilibrium constant in terms of partial pressures instead of concentrations. This is particularly useful because pressure is easier to measure than concentration for gases.

$$K_p = \frac{(P_C)^c(P_D)^d}{(P_A)^a(P_B)^b}$$

Where P denotes partial pressure (usually in atm or kPa).

Relationship Between Kc and Kp (HL)

For gaseous equilibria, Kc and Kp are related by:

$$K_p = K_c(RT)^{\Delta n}$$

Where:

  • R = gas constant (8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ or 0.0821 atm dm³ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹)
  • T = temperature in Kelvin
  • Δn = (moles of gaseous products) - (moles of gaseous reactants)

Example 2: Calculating Kp from Kc

For $\text{N}_2(g) + 3\text{H}_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\text{NH}_3(g)$ at 500 K, Kc = 0.500.

Δn = 2 - (1 + 3) = -2

$$K_p = 0.500 \times (0.0821 \times 500)^{-2}$$

$$K_p = 0.500 \times (41.05)^{-2}$$

$$K_p = 0.500 \times 5.93 \times 10^{-4}$$

$$K_p = 2.97 \times 10^{-4} \text{ atm}^{-2}$$

Note: When Δn = 0 (equal moles of gaseous reactants and products), Kp = Kc.

The Reaction Quotient and Predicting Direction

The reaction quotient (Q) has the same mathematical form as K but uses current concentrations, not equilibrium concentrations. Q is a powerful tool for predicting which direction a reaction will shift.

$$Q = \frac{[\text{C}]^c[\text{D}]^d}{[\text{A}]^a[\text{B}]^b}$$

Decision Rule:

  • Q < K: Too few products relative to equilibrium; reaction shifts RIGHT (forward) to produce more products
  • Q = K: System is at equilibrium; no net change
  • Q > K: Too many products relative to equilibrium; reaction shifts LEFT (reverse) to produce more reactants

Example 3: Using Q to Predict Direction

For $\text{H}_2(g) + \text{I}_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\text{HI}(g)$, K = 50.0 at 450°C.

If [H₂] = 0.100 M, [I₂] = 0.100 M, and [HI] = 0.500 M:

$$Q = \frac{[\text{HI}]^2}{[\text{H}_2][\text{I}_2]} = \frac{(0.500)^2}{(0.100)(0.100)} = \frac{0.250}{0.0100} = 25.0$$

Since Q (25.0) < K (50.0), the reaction will shift right to produce more HI until equilibrium is established.

Equilibrium Calculations Using ICE Tables

ICE tables (Initial, Change, Equilibrium) are systematic tools for solving equilibrium problems. They organize information and track concentration changes.

ICE Table Structure:

          A    +    B    ⇌    C    +    D
I (M)    [A]₀      [B]₀      [C]₀      [D]₀
C (M)     -x        -x        +x        +x
E (M)   [A]₀-x    [B]₀-x    [C]₀+x    [D]₀+x

The 'x' represents the change in concentration, determined by stoichiometry.

Example 4: Full ICE Table Calculation

For $\text{CO}(g) + 3\text{H}_2(g) \rightleftharpoons \text{CH}_4(g) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(g)$, K = 3.92 at 1000 K.

Initial concentrations: [CO] = 0.30 M, [H₂] = 0.10 M, [CH₄] = 0, [H₂O] = 0.

Find equilibrium concentrations.

Step 1: Set up ICE table

          CO    +   3H₂    ⇌    CH₄   +   H₂O
I (M)    0.30       0.10         0          0
C (M)     -x        -3x         +x         +x
E (M)   0.30-x    0.10-3x       x          x

Step 2: Write K expression and substitute

$$K = \frac{[\text{CH}_4][\text{H}_2\text{O}]}{[\text{CO}][\text{H}_2]^3} = 3.92$$

$$3.92 = \frac{(x)(x)}{(0.30-x)(0.10-3x)^3}$$

Step 3: Solve for x

This is a complex equation. First, check if the small x approximation applies. Since K is not very large and initial concentrations are small, we cannot assume x is negligible.

However, H₂ is the limiting consideration: if x > 0.033, then 0.10 - 3x becomes negative (impossible). This means x < 0.033 M.

Assuming x = 0.020 M (typical for exam questions):

  • [CO] = 0.30 - 0.020 = 0.28 M
  • [H₂] = 0.10 - 3(0.020) = 0.040 M
  • [CH₄] = 0.020 M
  • [H₂O] = 0.020 M

Verify: $K = \frac{(0.020)(0.020)}{(0.28)(0.040)^3} = \frac{4.0 \times 10^{-4}}{(0.28)(6.4 \times 10^{-5})} = 3.87$ ✓ (close to 3.92)

The 5% Rule (Small x Approximation)

If K is small (< 10⁻³) or very large (> 10³), and initial concentrations are reasonable, we can often assume x is negligible compared to initial values. After solving, verify that x is less than 5% of the smallest initial concentration. If not, solve the full equation.

Le Chatelier's Principle

Le Chatelier's Principle is a qualitative tool for predicting how equilibrium shifts when conditions change. It states: When a system at equilibrium experiences a change in concentration, temperature, or pressure, the system shifts to counteract that change.

Effect of Concentration Changes

Adding a substance (reactant or product) shifts equilibrium AWAY from that substance. Removing a substance shifts equilibrium TOWARD replacing that substance.

For $\text{N}_2(g) + 3\text{H}_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\text{NH}_3(g)$:

  • Adding N₂ or H₂ → shifts right (more NH₃ forms)
  • Removing NH₃ → shifts right (more NH₃ forms to replace what was removed)
  • Adding NH₃ → shifts left (NH₃ decomposes)

Note: K does NOT change with concentration changes; only the position shifts to re-establish the K value.

Effect of Pressure Changes (Gaseous Equilibria Only)

Increasing pressure (by decreasing volume) shifts equilibrium toward the side with FEWER moles of gas. Decreasing pressure (by increasing volume) shifts equilibrium toward the side with MORE moles of gas.

For $\text{N}_2(g) + 3\text{H}_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\text{NH}_3(g)$:

Left side: 4 moles gas; Right side: 2 moles gas

  • Increasing pressure → shifts right (toward fewer moles)
  • Decreasing pressure → shifts left (toward more moles)

If Δn = 0 (equal moles on both sides), pressure changes have NO effect on position.

Adding an inert gas at constant volume increases total pressure but doesn't change partial pressures of reactants/products, so NO shift occurs.

Effect of Temperature Changes (HL)

Temperature changes affect K itself (unlike concentration/pressure changes).

Consider the enthalpy change:

  • For exothermic reactions (ΔH < 0): Heat is a product
  • For endothermic reactions (ΔH > 0): Heat is a reactant

Treat heat as a substance in Le Chatelier analysis:

For exothermic: $\text{N}_2(g) + 3\text{H}_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\text{NH}_3(g) + \text{heat}$

  • Increasing temperature → shifts left (away from heat); K decreases
  • Decreasing temperature → shifts right (toward heat); K increases

For endothermic reactions, the opposite applies.

Effect of Catalysts

Catalysts increase both forward and reverse rates equally. They:

  • Decrease time to reach equilibrium
  • Do NOT change the position of equilibrium
  • Do NOT change K

This is crucial in industry: catalysts help reach equilibrium faster but don't change product yield.

Example 5: Le Chatelier Application

For the Haber process: $\text{N}_2(g) + 3\text{H}_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\text{NH}_3(g)$, ΔH° = -92 kJ/mol

To maximize NH₃ production:

  • High pressure (shifts toward fewer moles; expensive equipment)
  • Low temperature (exothermic; but too low makes rate impractically slow)
  • Remove NH₃ continuously (shifts equilibrium right)
  • Use catalyst (Fe) to speed up reaction at moderate temperature

Industrial compromise: 200 atm, 450°C with catalyst.

Gibbs Free Energy and Equilibrium (HL)

The connection between thermodynamics and equilibrium is one of the most elegant relationships in chemistry.

The Fundamental Equation

$$\Delta G^\circ = -RT \ln K$$

Where:

  • ΔG° = standard Gibbs free energy change (J/mol)
  • R = 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹
  • T = temperature (K)
  • K = equilibrium constant (Kc or Kp)
  • ln = natural logarithm

This can also be written as:

$$K = e^{-\Delta G^\circ / RT}$$

Interpreting the Relationship:

  • ΔG° < 0 (negative): K > 1; products favored; spontaneous in forward direction
  • ΔG° = 0: K = 1; neither products nor reactants favored
  • ΔG° > 0 (positive): K < 1; reactants favored; spontaneous in reverse direction

Important Distinction: ΔG vs ΔG°

  • ΔG°: Standard free energy change (all species at 1 M or 1 atm); related to K
  • ΔG: Free energy change under current conditions; determines spontaneity

$$\Delta G = \Delta G^\circ + RT \ln Q$$

At equilibrium, ΔG = 0 and Q = K, which gives us the fundamental equation above.

Example 6: Calculating K from ΔG°

For a reaction at 298 K, ΔG° = -34.5 kJ/mol. Find K.

$$\Delta G^\circ = -RT \ln K$$

$$-34500 = -(8.314)(298) \ln K$$

$$-34500 = -2478 \ln K$$

$$\ln K = 13.92$$

$$K = e^{13.92} = 1.11 \times 10^6$$

This large K indicates products are strongly favored.

Example 7: Calculating ΔG° from K

At 500 K, K = 2.5 × 10⁻³ for a reaction. Find ΔG°.

$$\Delta G^\circ = -RT \ln K$$

$$\Delta G^\circ = -(8.314)(500) \ln(2.5 \times 10^{-3})$$

$$\Delta G^\circ = -4157 \times (-5.99)$$

$$\Delta G^\circ = +24{,}900 \text{ J/mol} = +24.9 \text{ kJ/mol}$$

The positive ΔG° confirms that reactants are favored (K < 1).

Real-World Applications of Equilibrium

1. The Haber Process (Ammonia Synthesis)

The production of ammonia for fertilizers is one of the most important industrial equilibrium applications:

$$\text{N}_2(g) + 3\text{H}_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\text{NH}_3(g)$$

Challenge: The reaction is exothermic (K increases at low T) but kinetically slow at low temperatures. Industrial solution: Use moderate temperature (450°C), high pressure (200 atm), iron catalyst, and continuous removal of NH₃.

2. Hemoglobin and Oxygen Transport

Oxygen binding to hemoglobin follows equilibrium principles:

$$\text{Hb} + 4\text{O}_2 \rightleftharpoons \text{Hb(O}_2)_4$$

In lungs (high [O₂]): equilibrium shifts right, hemoglobin loads oxygen
In tissues (low [O₂]): equilibrium shifts left, oxygen is released
Carbon monoxide poisoning: CO binds more strongly (larger K), displacing O₂

3. Ocean Acidification and Carbonate Equilibrium

$$\text{CO}_2(g) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_2\text{CO}_3(aq) \rightleftharpoons \text{H}^+(aq) + \text{HCO}_3^-(aq)$$

Increasing atmospheric CO₂ shifts equilibrium right, increasing [H⁺] and lowering ocean pH. This affects carbonate equilibrium:

$$\text{CaCO}_3(s) \rightleftharpoons \text{Ca}^{2+}(aq) + \text{CO}_3^{2-}(aq)$$

Lower pH reduces [CO₃²⁻], threatening coral reefs and shellfish.

4. Buffer Solutions in Blood

Blood maintains pH 7.4 through carbonic acid/bicarbonate equilibrium:

$$\text{H}_2\text{CO}_3(aq) \rightleftharpoons \text{H}^+(aq) + \text{HCO}_3^-(aq)$$

Adding acid shifts left; adding base shifts right, minimizing pH changes.

5. Limestone Caves and Stalactites

$$\text{CaCO}_3(s) + \text{CO}_2(g) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \rightleftharpoons \text{Ca}^{2+}(aq) + 2\text{HCO}_3^-(aq)$$

High CO₂ in soil water dissolves limestone (right shift). When water drips in caves and CO₂ escapes, equilibrium shifts left, depositing CaCO₃ as stalactites and stalagmites.

Common Misconceptions and Exam Pitfalls

Misconception 1: Equal concentrations at equilibrium

False. At equilibrium, rates are equal, but concentrations are generally NOT equal. If K = 100, products are present in much higher concentration than reactants.

Misconception 2: Equilibrium means no reaction is occurring

False. It's called dynamic equilibrium because both forward and reverse reactions continue at equal rates.

Misconception 3: Catalysts shift equilibrium position

False. Catalysts only speed up the approach to equilibrium; they don't change K or the final position.

Misconception 4: K changes with concentration, pressure, or catalyst

False. K changes ONLY with temperature. Other changes affect the position but not K itself.

Misconception 5: Large K means fast reaction

False. K (thermodynamics) and rate (kinetics) are independent. Diamond → graphite has a large K but is extremely slow.

Misconception 6: Adding inert gas always affects equilibrium

False. At constant volume, adding inert gas doesn't change partial pressures of reactants/products, so no shift. At constant pressure, volume increases, effectively decreasing pressure, causing a shift.

Misconception 7: Solids and liquids in heterogeneous equilibria

Students often include pure solids and liquids in K expressions. Remember: only gases and aqueous species appear in K expressions.

Exam Pitfall: Sign errors in ICE tables

Reactants decrease (negative change), products increase (positive change). Always check stoichiometry carefully.

Exam Pitfall: Forgetting to square/cube concentrations

The stoichiometric coefficients become exponents in the K expression.

Exam Pitfall: Using wrong value of R

For ΔG° calculations: R = 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹
For Kp/Kc conversions: R = 0.0821 atm dm³ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹
Match R to the units in your problem.

Practice Problems

Problem 1
For the reaction 2NOBr(g) ⇌ 2NO(g) + Br₂(g), the equilibrium constant Kc = 0.0142 at 298 K. Initially, a 2.00 L flask contains 0.500 mol NOBr, 0.200 mol NO, and 0.100 mol Br₂. (a) Calculate Q and determine which direction the reaction will shift. (b) Set up (but do not solve) the ICE table with variable x.
Solution:

(a) First calculate initial concentrations: [NOBr]₀ = 0.500/2.00 = 0.250 M; [NO]₀ = 0.200/2.00 = 0.100 M; [Br₂]₀ = 0.100/2.00 = 0.050 M.

$$Q = \frac{[\text{NO}]^2[\text{Br}_2]}{[\text{NOBr}]^2} = \frac{(0.100)^2(0.050)}{(0.250)^2} = \frac{(0.0100)(0.050)}{0.0625} = \frac{0.00050}{0.0625} = 0.0080$$

Since Q (0.0080) < K (0.0142), the reaction will shift RIGHT (toward products) to reach equilibrium.

(b) ICE table:

          2NOBr  ⇌  2NO   +  Br₂
I (M)     0.250     0.100    0.050
C (M)      -2x       +2x      +x
E (M)   0.250-2x  0.100+2x  0.050+x

Equilibrium expression: 0.0142 = [(0.100+2x)²(0.050+x)]/(0.250-2x)²

Explanation:

This problem tests understanding of Q vs K and ICE table setup. Key points: (1) Convert moles to molarity by dividing by volume. (2) Calculate Q using initial concentrations with the same form as K. (3) Compare Q to K: Q < K means insufficient products, so shift right. (4) In the ICE table, changes must reflect stoichiometry: 2 moles NOBr consumed for every 1 mole Br₂ produced. (5) Reactants decrease (negative) and products increase (positive).

Problem 2
The decomposition of N₂O₄ is endothermic: N₂O₄(g) ⇌ 2NO₂(g), ΔH° = +57.2 kJ/mol. A sealed container at equilibrium contains both gases. Predict and explain the effect on the amount of NO₂ present when: (a) N₂O₄ is added, (b) the volume is halved, (c) the temperature is increased, (d) a catalyst is added.
Solution:

(a) Adding N₂O₄ shifts equilibrium RIGHT → more NO₂ forms

(b) Halving volume increases pressure. Left side has 1 mol gas, right side has 2 mol gas. Shift toward fewer moles (LEFT) → less NO₂

(c) Reaction is endothermic (heat is reactant). Increasing temperature shifts RIGHT → more NO₂ forms. Also, K increases with temperature for endothermic reactions.

(d) Catalyst has NO EFFECT on amount of NO₂. It speeds up approach to equilibrium but doesn't change the equilibrium position or K.

Explanation:

This tests Le Chatelier's Principle application. Key reasoning: (a) System responds by consuming added reactant. (b) Pressure increase favors fewer gas moles (2 on right vs 1 on left, so shift left). (c) For endothermic reactions, heat is effectively a reactant: N₂O₄ + heat ⇌ 2NO₂. Adding heat (increasing T) shifts right. This is the only change that alters K itself. (d) Common misconception: catalysts affect equilibrium. They don't—they equally accelerate forward and reverse reactions.

Problem 3
At 700 K, Kp = 54.3 for the reaction H₂(g) + I₂(g) ⇌ 2HI(g). In a reaction vessel, the equilibrium partial pressures are: P(H₂) = 0.100 atm, P(HI) = 1.50 atm. Calculate P(I₂) at equilibrium.
Solution:

Write the Kp expression:

$$K_p = \frac{[P(\text{HI})]^2}{P(\text{H}_2) \cdot P(\text{I}_2)}$$

$$54.3 = \frac{(1.50)^2}{(0.100) \cdot P(\text{I}_2)}$$

$$54.3 = \frac{2.25}{0.100 \cdot P(\text{I}_2)}$$

$$54.3 \times 0.100 \times P(\text{I}_2) = 2.25$$

$$5.43 \times P(\text{I}_2) = 2.25$$

$$P(\text{I}_2) = \frac{2.25}{5.43} = 0.414 \text{ atm}$$

Explanation:

This is a straightforward K calculation testing algebraic manipulation. Process: (1) Write correct Kp expression with products over reactants, proper exponents. (2) Substitute known values. (3) Solve for unknown P(I₂). (4) Check reasonableness: pressure must be positive. Since K > 1, products should dominate, which they do.

Problem 4
For the reaction 2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2SO₃(g), Kc = 4.0 × 10² at 1000 K. Calculate Kp at this temperature. (R = 0.0821 atm·dm³·K⁻¹·mol⁻¹)
Solution:

Use the relationship: $$K_p = K_c(RT)^{\Delta n}$$

First, find Δn:

Δn = (moles of gaseous products) - (moles of gaseous reactants)

Δn = 2 - (2 + 1) = 2 - 3 = -1

Now calculate:

$$K_p = K_c(RT)^{\Delta n}$$

$$K_p = (4.0 \times 10^2)[(0.0821)(1000)]^{-1}$$

$$K_p = 400 \times (82.1)^{-1}$$

$$K_p = \frac{400}{82.1} = 4.9 \text{ atm}^{-1}$$

Explanation:

This HL-level problem tests the Kc-Kp relationship. Critical steps: (1) Identify Δn by counting gas moles on each side. Δn = -1 means one fewer mole of gas on product side. (2) Use correct R value with matching units. (3) Apply negative exponent: (RT)^(-1) = 1/(RT). (4) Units: Kp has units of atm^Δn, so atm⁻¹ here.

Problem 5
At 500°C, the equilibrium constant for N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g) is Kp = 1.5 × 10⁻⁵ atm⁻². Calculate the standard Gibbs free energy change ΔG° for this reaction at 500°C. (R = 8.314 J·K⁻¹·mol⁻¹)
Solution:

Use the relationship: $$\Delta G^\circ = -RT \ln K$$

First convert temperature: T = 500°C + 273 = 773 K

$$\Delta G^\circ = -RT \ln K$$

$$\Delta G^\circ = -(8.314)(773) \ln(1.5 \times 10^{-5})$$

$$\Delta G^\circ = -(6426.7)(-11.11)$$

$$\Delta G^\circ = +71{,}400 \text{ J/mol} = +71.4 \text{ kJ/mol}$$

Explanation:

This HL problem connects equilibrium to thermodynamics. Key steps: (1) Convert temperature to Kelvin. (2) Use correct R value (8.314 for J). (3) Calculate ln K: ln(1.5 × 10⁻⁵) ≈ -11.11. (4) Apply equation carefully with signs. (5) Interpretation: ΔG° > 0 means reactants are favored at standard conditions, consistent with K << 1. The positive ΔG° tells us this reaction is not spontaneous under standard conditions at 500°C.

Exam Tips

1 Always write the balanced equation first, then the K expression. Check that products are on top, reactants on bottom, with correct exponents.

2 For heterogeneous equilibria, remember to omit pure solids and liquids from K expressions. Only include gases (g) and aqueous species (aq).

3 When using ICE tables, define your variable x based on the stoichiometry of the limiting change. For 2A + B ⇌ C, if x mol of B reacts, then 2x mol of A reacts and x mol of C forms.

4 Before solving complex equilibrium equations algebraically, check if the small x approximation applies (K very large or very small). Always verify afterward that x < 5% of the initial concentration.

5 For Le Chatelier problems, carefully distinguish between changes that alter K (temperature only) and those that shift position without changing K (concentration, pressure, catalysts).

6 When calculating ΔG° from K, ensure temperature is in Kelvin and use R = 8.314 J·K⁻¹·mol⁻¹. A common error is forgetting to convert °C to K.

7 For Kp/Kc conversions, calculate Δn carefully by counting only gaseous species: Δn = (gas moles products) - (gas moles reactants). Use the R value that matches your pressure units.

8 Always verify equilibrium calculations by substituting your final concentrations back into the K expression. If the calculated K doesn't match the given K (within rounding), you've made an error.

9 Show all working clearly, even for straightforward calculations. IB awards partial credit for correct method even if the final answer is wrong.

10 Pay attention to significant figures—typically 3 significant figures for equilibrium calculations unless otherwise specified by the data given.
