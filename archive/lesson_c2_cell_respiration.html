Key Definitions

Cell Respiration
The controlled release of energy from organic compounds to synthesize ATP through oxidation reactions
ATP (Adenosine Triphosphate)
Universal energy currency consisting of adenosine bonded to three phosphate groups; energy released when terminal phosphate is hydrolyzed
Glycolysis
Metabolic pathway converting glucose to two pyruvate molecules, yielding net 2 ATP and 2 NADH in the cytoplasm
Krebs Cycle
Cyclical metabolic pathway in mitochondrial matrix oxidizing acetyl-CoA to CO2, producing reduced coenzymes and ATP
Oxidative Phosphorylation
ATP synthesis driven by electron transport chain creating proton gradient across inner mitochondrial membrane
Chemiosmosis
ATP synthesis via proton flow through ATP synthase driven by electrochemical gradient
Substrate-level Phosphorylation
Direct ATP synthesis by transfer of phosphate from substrate to ADP during glycolysis and Krebs cycle
Anaerobic Respiration
ATP production without oxygen, using glycolysis followed by fermentation to regenerate NAD+

Introduction: Why Cells Need Energy

All living cells require continuous energy input to maintain life processes. This energy powers active transport, protein synthesis, DNA replication, cell division, muscle contraction, and maintaining body temperature. While sunlight provides energy for photosynthetic organisms, all cells—including plant cells—rely on cell respiration to convert chemical energy stored in organic molecules into ATP.

The Central Role of ATP

ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is the universal energy currency of cells. Its structure consists of:

  • Adenine (nitrogenous base)
  • Ribose (5-carbon sugar)
  • Three phosphate groups linked by high-energy bonds

Energy is released when ATP is hydrolyzed to ADP (adenosine diphosphate) and inorganic phosphate:

ATP + H₂O → ADP + Pᵢ + energy (30.5 kJ/mol)

This reaction is catalyzed by ATPase enzymes. The energy released is used to drive endergonic (energy-requiring) reactions in cells. ATP is ideal as an energy currency because:

  1. It releases energy in small, manageable amounts
  2. It is rapidly regenerated (recycled 300-400 times daily)
  3. It is mobile and can diffuse to where energy is needed
  4. The hydrolysis reaction is reversible

Overview of Cell Respiration

Cell respiration is the controlled release of energy from organic compounds to form ATP. The complete oxidation of glucose follows the equation:

C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy

This process occurs in four main stages:

  1. Glycolysis (cytoplasm) - glucose → pyruvate
  2. Link reaction (mitochondrial matrix) - pyruvate → acetyl-CoA
  3. Krebs cycle (mitochondrial matrix) - acetyl-CoA → CO₂ + reduced coenzymes
  4. Oxidative phosphorylation (inner mitochondrial membrane) - reduced coenzymes → ATP

Glycolysis: The First Stage

Glycolysis occurs in the cytoplasm and does not require oxygen, making it the universal first step in both aerobic and anaerobic respiration. The pathway converts one glucose molecule (6 carbons) into two pyruvate molecules (3 carbons each).

The Two Phases of Glycolysis

Phase 1: Energy Investment (Steps 1-5)
Glucose is phosphorylated twice, using 2 ATP, making it more reactive and trapping it in the cell. The 6-carbon molecule is then split into two 3-carbon molecules (triose phosphates).

Phase 2: Energy Payoff (Steps 6-10)
Each triose phosphate is oxidized and converted to pyruvate. This produces:

  • 2 ATP per triose phosphate (4 ATP total) via substrate-level phosphorylation
  • 1 NADH per triose phosphate (2 NADH total) via dehydrogenation

Net Yield from Glycolysis:

  • ATP: 4 produced - 2 consumed = 2 ATP net
  • NADH: 2 NADH
  • Pyruvate: 2 pyruvate

Key Enzymes:

  • Hexokinase (step 1): phosphorylates glucose
  • Phosphofructokinase (step 3): rate-limiting enzyme, regulated by ATP/ADP ratio
  • Pyruvate kinase (step 10): catalyzes final ATP generation

Why Glycolysis Matters:

Glycolysis is evolutionarily ancient and occurs in virtually all organisms. It can proceed anaerobically, allowing ATP production when oxygen is limited. However, it produces only 2 ATP per glucose—about 2% of glucose's total energy content. The majority of energy remains stored in pyruvate and NADH.

Link Reaction: Connecting to the Krebs Cycle

After glycolysis, pyruvate must be transported into mitochondria for further oxidation. The link reaction (also called pyruvate oxidation or transition reaction) occurs in the mitochondrial matrix and connects glycolysis to the Krebs cycle.

The Link Reaction Process:

For each pyruvate molecule:

Pyruvate (3C) + CoA + NAD⁺ → Acetyl-CoA (2C) + CO₂ + NADH

This reaction involves three simultaneous changes:

  1. Decarboxylation: removal of CO₂ (reduces 3C to 2C)
  2. Dehydrogenation: removal of hydrogen (reduces NAD⁺ to NADH)
  3. Combination with coenzyme A: forms acetyl-CoA

The enzyme complex catalyzing this is pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, one of the largest enzyme complexes known.

Yield from Link Reaction (per glucose):
Since 2 pyruvate are produced from glucose:

  • 2 acetyl-CoA
  • 2 NADH
  • 2 CO₂

Regulation:

Pyruvate dehydrogenase is inhibited by high ATP and acetyl-CoA (negative feedback) and activated by high ADP and pyruvate. This ensures the cell only oxidizes pyruvate when energy is needed.

Irreversibility:

The link reaction is irreversible under physiological conditions. Once pyruvate is decarboxylated to acetyl-CoA, it cannot be converted back to glucose. This is why fatty acids (which break down to acetyl-CoA) cannot be used to synthesize glucose in animals.

Krebs Cycle: Complete Oxidation

The Krebs cycle (citric acid cycle or TCA cycle) occurs in the mitochondrial matrix. It completely oxidizes the 2-carbon acetyl group from acetyl-CoA to 2CO₂, capturing energy in reduced coenzymes.

The Cycle Steps:

  1. Acetyl-CoA (2C) + Oxaloacetate (4C) → Citrate (6C) + CoA
    Enzyme: citrate synthase
  2. Citrate (6C) → Isocitrate (6C) → α-ketoglutarate (5C) + CO₂ + NADH
    First decarboxylation and dehydrogenation
  3. α-ketoglutarate (5C) → Succinyl-CoA (4C) + CO₂ + NADH
    Second decarboxylation and dehydrogenation
  4. Succinyl-CoA (4C) → Succinate (4C) + ATP (or GTP)
    Substrate-level phosphorylation
  5. Succinate (4C) → Fumarate (4C) + FADH₂
    Dehydrogenation using FAD
  6. Fumarate (4C) → Malate (4C) → Oxaloacetate (4C) + NADH
    Final dehydrogenation, regenerating oxaloacetate

Yield per Acetyl-CoA (one turn):

  • 3 NADH
  • 1 FADH₂
  • 1 ATP (or GTP, equivalent)
  • 2 CO₂

Yield per Glucose (two turns):

  • 6 NADH
  • 2 FADH₂
  • 2 ATP
  • 4 CO₂

Why the Cycle is Cyclical:

Oxaloacetate is regenerated at the end, allowing continuous operation as long as acetyl-CoA is supplied. The cycle is amphibolic—it functions in both catabolism (breaking down) and anabolism (building up), providing intermediates for biosynthesis.

Regulation:

Key regulatory enzymes (citrate synthase, isocitrate dehydrogenase, α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase) are inhibited by high ATP and NADH ratios, preventing wasteful oxidation when energy is abundant.

Oxidative Phosphorylation: The Major ATP Source

Oxidative phosphorylation occurs at the inner mitochondrial membrane and produces the majority of ATP in aerobic respiration. It consists of two coupled processes: the electron transport chain (ETC) and chemiosmosis.

The Electron Transport Chain

The ETC consists of four protein complexes (I, II, III, IV) embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane, plus two mobile electron carriers (ubiquinone/coenzyme Q and cytochrome c).

NADH Pathway:
NADH → Complex I → Ubiquinone → Complex III → Cytochrome c → Complex IV → O₂

  • Protons pumped at Complexes I, III, and IV
  • NADH delivers electrons at higher energy level

FADH₂ Pathway:
FADH₂ → Complex II → Ubiquinone → Complex III → Cytochrome c → Complex IV → O₂

  • Protons pumped at Complexes III and IV only
  • FADH₂ bypasses Complex I (less proton pumping)

As electrons pass through the chain, they lose energy. This energy pumps protons (H⁺) from the mitochondrial matrix into the intermembrane space, creating an electrochemical gradient (proton-motive force).

Chemiosmosis and ATP Synthesis

The proton gradient represents stored potential energy. Protons flow back through ATP synthase (Complex V), a remarkable molecular motor. As protons flow through the F₀ subunit, it rotates the F₁ subunit, which catalyzes:

ADP + Pᵢ → ATP

This process, called chemiosmosis, couples the exergonic flow of protons to the endergonic synthesis of ATP.

Oxygen's Essential Role

Oxygen is the final electron acceptor:

½O₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂O

Without oxygen, electrons cannot exit the chain, NADH and FADH₂ cannot be reoxidized, and NAD⁺ and FAD cannot be regenerated for glycolysis and Krebs cycle. Aerobic respiration stops.

ATP Yield from Oxidative Phosphorylation:

  • Each NADH generates approximately 2.5 ATP
  • Each FADH₂ generates approximately 1.5 ATP

These are average values; the actual ratio depends on proton pumping efficiency and the H⁺/ATP ratio of ATP synthase.

Total ATP Yield: Theoretical vs Actual

Theoretical Maximum ATP Yield from One Glucose:

From Glycolysis:

  • 2 ATP (substrate-level)
  • 2 NADH → 5 ATP (if transported via malate-aspartate shuttle) or 3 ATP (if via glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle)

From Link Reaction:

  • 2 NADH → 5 ATP

From Krebs Cycle:

  • 2 ATP (substrate-level)
  • 6 NADH → 15 ATP
  • 2 FADH₂ → 3 ATP
Theoretical Total (using malate-aspartate shuttle):
2 + 5 + 5 + 2 + 15 + 3 = 32 ATP
Theoretical Total (using glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle):
2 + 3 + 5 + 2 + 15 + 3 = 30 ATP

Why Actual Yields are Lower (typically 30-32 ATP):

  1. Proton Leak: Inner mitochondrial membrane is not perfectly impermeable; some protons leak across without passing through ATP synthase
  2. Transport Costs: ATP must be transported out of mitochondria in exchange for ADP (uses energy from proton gradient)
  3. NADH from Glycolysis: Produced in cytoplasm, must be transported into mitochondria via shuttle systems (costs energy)
  4. Variable H⁺/ATP Ratios: The number of protons required per ATP varies (approximately 4 H⁺ per ATP including transport costs)
  5. Alternative Uses: Proton gradient also powers other mitochondrial transport processes

Comparison with Older Textbooks:

Older sources cite 36-38 ATP per glucose using 3 ATP per NADH and 2 ATP per FADH₂. Modern research shows lower P/O ratios (ATP per oxygen atom), yielding 30-32 ATP. IB examinations accept values in the range of 30-32 ATP.

Anaerobic Respiration and Fermentation

When oxygen is absent or limited, cells cannot use oxidative phosphorylation. Glycolysis can continue only if NAD⁺ is regenerated to accept hydrogen. Fermentation achieves this by using pyruvate or its derivatives as the final electron acceptor.

Lactate Fermentation (Animals, Some Bacteria)

Occurs in muscle cells during intense exercise when oxygen supply is insufficient:

Pyruvate + NADH → Lactate + NAD⁺

Catalyzed by lactate dehydrogenase. The process:

  1. Regenerates NAD⁺ for glycolysis to continue
  2. Produces no additional ATP (only glycolysis produces ATP)
  3. Creates lactate that accumulates in muscle and blood
  4. Is reversible—lactate is transported to liver and converted back to pyruvate when oxygen becomes available (Cori cycle)

Ethanol Fermentation (Yeast, Some Bacteria)

Used in brewing and baking:

Pyruvate → Ethanal + CO₂
Ethanal + NADH → Ethanol + NAD⁺

Two-step process:

  1. Decarboxylation: pyruvate → ethanal + CO₂ (pyruvate decarboxylase)
  2. Reduction: ethanal + NADH → ethanol + NAD⁺ (alcohol dehydrogenase)

Regenerates NAD⁺ for glycolysis. CO₂ makes bread rise; ethanol provides intoxicating effect in beverages.

Comparison: Aerobic vs Anaerobic

Feature Aerobic Anaerobic (Lactate) Anaerobic (Ethanol)
Location Cytoplasm + mitochondria Cytoplasm only Cytoplasm only
O₂ required Yes No No
ATP per glucose 30-32 2 2
Products CO₂, H₂O Lactate Ethanol, CO₂
Reversible No Yes (lactate → pyruvate) No

Efficiency:

Anaerobic respiration captures only ~2% of glucose's energy (2 ATP vs 30-32 ATP). The remaining energy remains in lactate or ethanol. This is highly inefficient but allows rapid ATP production when oxygen is limited.

Measuring Respiration Rates

Respiration rates can be measured experimentally using respirometers, which detect changes in gas volume or pressure as organisms respire.

Simple Respirometer Design

A typical respirometer consists of:

  • Sealed chamber containing organism (seeds, insects, small animal)
  • Soda lime or potassium hydroxide to absorb CO₂ produced
  • Capillary tube with colored fluid to measure volume change
  • Water bath to maintain constant temperature

Principle:

During aerobic respiration:

  • O₂ is consumed
  • CO₂ is produced but absorbed by soda lime
  • Net decrease in gas volume
  • Colored fluid moves toward chamber

The rate of fluid movement indicates the rate of O₂ consumption, which reflects respiration rate.

Calculating Respiration Rate:

Respiration rate = Volume of O₂ consumed (mm³) / [Time (min) × Mass of organism (g)]

Units: mm³ O₂ g⁻¹ min⁻¹

Respiratory Quotient (RQ):

RQ indicates which substrate is being respired:

RQ = CO₂ produced / O₂ consumed
  • Carbohydrates: RQ = 1.0 (C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O)
  • Lipids: RQ ≈ 0.7 (more O₂ needed per CO₂ produced)
  • Proteins: RQ ≈ 0.9
  • Anaerobic: RQ = ∞ (CO₂ produced without O₂ consumption)

Measuring RQ:

Requires two respirometers:

  1. With CO₂ absorber (measures O₂ consumption only)
  2. Without CO₂ absorber (measures net gas exchange: O₂ consumed - CO₂ produced)

Experimental Controls:

  • Control respirometer with no organism (accounts for pressure/temperature changes)
  • Constant temperature (temperature affects gas volume and metabolic rate)
  • Same mass/number of organisms
  • Equilibration period before measurements

Sources of Error:

  • Temperature fluctuations
  • Incomplete CO₂ absorption
  • Organism movement affecting readings
  • Leaks in apparatus
  • Anaerobic respiration occurring (produces CO₂ without consuming O₂)

Real-World Applications

Medicine and Exercise Physiology

Understanding cell respiration is crucial for managing metabolic diseases:

  • Diabetes: Impaired glucose uptake affects glycolysis
  • Mitochondrial diseases: Mutations in ETC proteins cause severe disorders
  • Athletic performance: Training increases mitochondrial density, improving aerobic capacity
  • Altitude adaptation: Low oxygen stimulates red blood cell production

Biotechnology and Industry

  • Brewing and baking: Exploits yeast ethanol fermentation
  • Yogurt production: Bacterial lactate fermentation
  • Biofuel production: Engineered microorganisms maximize ethanol output
  • Bioprocessing: Controlling oxygen levels optimizes product yields

Agriculture

  • Seed storage: Low O₂ atmospheres reduce respiration, preserving viability
  • Fruit storage: Controlled atmosphere storage slows ripening
  • Grain storage: Monitoring CO₂ levels detects pest infestation or spoilage

Environmental Science

  • Soil respiration: Indicates microbial activity and carbon cycling
  • Aquatic ecosystems: Dissolved oxygen levels determine organism survival
  • Climate change: Increased soil respiration releases CO₂, creating feedback loops

Forensic Science

  • Time of death estimation: Temperature and metabolic changes
  • Decomposition rates: Affected by oxygen availability and temperature

Common Misconceptions

Misconception 1: "Oxygen is needed for glycolysis"
Reality: Glycolysis is anaerobic and occurs in the cytoplasm. Oxygen is only required for the ETC. However, without oxygen, NADH from glycolysis cannot be reoxidized via oxidative phosphorylation, so fermentation must regenerate NAD⁺.
Misconception 2: "ATP is produced only in mitochondria"
Reality: Glycolysis produces 2 ATP in the cytoplasm via substrate-level phosphorylation. The majority of ATP (from oxidative phosphorylation) is produced in mitochondria, but not all.
Misconception 3: "All 38 ATP come from one glucose"
Reality: Older textbooks cite 38 ATP (gross) or 36 ATP (net). Modern values are 30-32 ATP due to updated P/O ratios and accounting for transport costs.
Misconception 4: "Fermentation produces energy"
Reality: Fermentation itself produces no ATP. Its purpose is to regenerate NAD⁺ so glycolysis can continue producing 2 ATP per glucose. The overall yield remains 2 ATP from glycolysis.
Misconception 5: "Lactate causes muscle soreness"
Reality: Lactate is cleared rapidly (within an hour). Delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS) is caused by micro-tears in muscle fibers and inflammation, not lactate accumulation.
Misconception 6: "The Krebs cycle directly produces most ATP"
Reality: Krebs cycle produces only 2 ATP directly (substrate-level phosphorylation). Its primary function is generating reduced coenzymes (6 NADH + 2 FADH₂) that drive oxidative phosphorylation to produce ~24 ATP.
Misconception 7: "CO₂ is produced throughout cell respiration"
Reality: CO₂ is produced only in: Link reaction (1 CO₂ per pyruvate), Krebs cycle (2 CO₂ per acetyl-CoA), and Ethanol fermentation (1 CO₂ per pyruvate). Glycolysis and lactate fermentation produce no CO₂.

Practice Problems

Problem 1
A student investigates aerobic respiration in germinating peas using a respirometer at 25°C. The colored fluid in the capillary tube moves 15 mm toward the chamber in 10 minutes. The bore of the capillary tube is 1 mm in diameter. The total mass of peas is 5.0 g. Calculate the rate of oxygen consumption per gram of peas per minute. Show your working.

Solution:

Working:

Volume of O₂ consumed = πr²h = π × (0.5)² × 15 = 11.78 mm³

Respiration rate = 11.78 mm³ / (10 min × 5.0 g) = 0.236 mm³ g⁻¹ min⁻¹

Explanation:

First calculate the volume using the cylinder formula V = πr²h. Radius is half the diameter (0.5 mm). Then divide by time and mass to get rate per gram per minute. This represents the O₂ consumption rate, which is proportional to aerobic respiration rate.

Problem 2
Complete oxidation of one glucose molecule produces 30 ATP. Of these, 4 ATP are produced by substrate-level phosphorylation. (a) Calculate how many ATP are produced by oxidative phosphorylation. (b) Calculate the percentage of total ATP produced by oxidative phosphorylation. (c) Explain why this demonstrates that oxidative phosphorylation is the major source of ATP.

Solution:

(a) 30 - 4 = 26 ATP from oxidative phosphorylation

(b) (26/30) × 100 = 86.7%

(c) Over 86% of ATP comes from oxidative phosphorylation, making it the dominant pathway for ATP production in aerobic conditions.

Explanation:

Substrate-level phosphorylation occurs in glycolysis (2 ATP) and Krebs cycle (2 ATP). All remaining ATP comes from oxidative phosphorylation driven by NADH and FADH₂ oxidation through the ETC. This question emphasizes the quantitative importance of the ETC and chemiosmosis.

Problem 3
A mutation in Complex I of the electron transport chain prevents NADH from being oxidized, but FADH₂ can still donate electrons at Complex II. Explain the effect this would have on: (a) ATP production from glucose, (b) the Krebs cycle, (c) glycolysis.

Solution:

(a) ATP production drastically reduced. NADH cannot be oxidized, losing ~25 ATP per glucose (10 NADH × 2.5 ATP each). Only FADH₂-derived ATP (~3 ATP) and substrate-level phosphorylation (~4 ATP) remain ≈ 7 ATP total.

(b) Krebs cycle would slow or stop. NAD⁺ cannot be regenerated, so dehydrogenation reactions cannot proceed. NADH accumulates.

(c) Glycolysis would slow. NADH from glycolysis cannot be reoxidized to NAD⁺ via oxidative phosphorylation, depleting cytoplasmic NAD⁺ needed for the glycolysis dehydrogenation step.

Explanation:

This question tests understanding of the interconnection between pathways. Without NAD⁺ regeneration, both glycolysis and Krebs cycle are inhibited despite adequate oxygen. This demonstrates that aerobic respiration requires functional ETC, not just oxygen availability.

Problem 4
During intense exercise, muscle cells produce lactate. (a) State the substrate and product of lactate fermentation. (b) Explain why lactate fermentation is necessary during intense exercise. (c) Describe what happens to lactate after exercise stops.

Solution:

(a) Substrate: pyruvate (and NADH). Product: lactate (and NAD⁺).

(b) During intense exercise, oxygen supply to muscles is insufficient for oxidative phosphorylation. NADH accumulates and NAD⁺ is depleted. Lactate fermentation regenerates NAD⁺ from NADH, allowing glycolysis to continue producing ATP (2 per glucose). Without NAD⁺ regeneration, glycolysis would stop and no ATP would be produced.

(c) Lactate diffuses into blood and is transported to the liver. In liver cells (with adequate oxygen), lactate is oxidized back to pyruvate by lactate dehydrogenase. Pyruvate then enters aerobic respiration or is converted to glucose via gluconeogenesis (Cori cycle).

Explanation:

This tests understanding of anaerobic respiration's role and reversibility. Key points: fermentation regenerates NAD⁺ (not produces ATP), it's temporary during O₂ deficit, and lactate is recycled rather than being waste. The Cori cycle demonstrates integration between different tissues.

Problem 5
A researcher measures gas exchange in a respirometer containing woodlice. In one hour, 180 mm³ of O₂ is consumed and 180 mm³ of CO₂ is produced (after correcting for the control). (a) Calculate the respiratory quotient (RQ). (b) Identify which type of substrate is primarily being respired. (c) Explain your reasoning.

Solution:

(a) RQ = CO₂ produced / O₂ consumed = 180/180 = 1.0

(b) Carbohydrates (sugars, starch, glycogen)

(c) An RQ of 1.0 indicates that one molecule of CO₂ is produced for each molecule of O₂ consumed. This matches the stoichiometry of carbohydrate oxidation: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O (ratio 6:6 = 1.0). Lipids have RQ ≈ 0.7 because they require more oxygen relative to CO₂ produced, and proteins have RQ ≈ 0.9.

Explanation:

RQ analysis is a powerful tool for identifying respiratory substrate. The balanced equation for glucose shows equal moles of O₂ and CO₂. Lipids have lower RQ because they contain less oxygen initially, requiring more O₂ for complete oxidation. Students should relate RQ values to substrate composition.

Problem 6
The table shows NADH and FADH₂ production at different stages of aerobic respiration of one glucose molecule. Calculate the maximum number of ATP that could be produced by oxidative phosphorylation, assuming 2.5 ATP per NADH and 1.5 ATP per FADH₂.
StageNADHFADH₂
Glycolysis20
Link reaction20
Krebs cycle62
Total102

Solution:

ATP from NADH: 10 × 2.5 = 25 ATP

ATP from FADH₂: 2 × 1.5 = 3 ATP

Total ATP from oxidative phosphorylation: 25 + 3 = 28 ATP

Explanation:

This calculation tests understanding of the relationship between reduced coenzymes and ATP yield. NADH yields more ATP than FADH₂ because NADH electrons enter the ETC at Complex I (higher energy level), allowing more proton pumping than FADH₂ electrons entering at Complex II. The 28 ATP, plus 4 ATP from substrate-level phosphorylation, gives approximately 32 ATP total (some may be lost to transport costs).

Exam Tips

  • Always show your working for calculations—partial credit is awarded even if final answer is wrong
  • When calculating ATP yield, state your assumptions (e.g., '2.5 ATP per NADH, 1.5 per FADH₂') as different values appear in different sources
  • For respirometer questions, remember to account for the control setup to correct for pressure/temperature changes
  • In compare/contrast questions, use a table or clear structure with both similarities AND differences
  • When asked to 'explain,' provide both the what (description) and why (reason/mechanism)—one-word answers rarely earn full marks
  • Know the exact locations: glycolysis in cytoplasm, link reaction and Krebs cycle in mitochondrial matrix, ETC on inner mitochondrial membrane
  • For anaerobic respiration, emphasize that fermentation regenerates NAD⁺, not produces ATP—the 2 ATP comes from glycolysis
  • In essay questions about respiration, use technical terms precisely: 'decarboxylation' not 'CO₂ removed,' 'dehydrogenation' not 'hydrogen removed'
  • Questions about poisons/inhibitors test understanding of interconnections—explain cascade effects (e.g., ETC blocked → NADH accumulates → Krebs cycle stops)
  • For RQ calculations, learn the values: carbohydrates = 1.0, lipids = 0.7, proteins = 0.9, anaerobic = infinity
  • Data-based questions often require you to calculate rates (per gram, per minute)—check units carefully
  • When drawing or labeling diagrams, include all required components: inner/outer membranes, matrix, intermembrane space, cristae, ATP synthase position
  • Common command terms: 'State' = no explanation needed, 'Explain' = reason/mechanism required, 'Calculate' = show working, 'Compare' = similarities AND differences
