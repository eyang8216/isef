Motion in Fields (D3)

Syllabus Code: D3 Level: HL Physics

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the concept of a field as a region where a force acts on an object
  • Calculate gravitational field strength and relate it to gravitational force
  • Apply Newton's law of gravitation to calculate forces between masses
  • Determine gravitational potential and gravitational potential energy in uniform and radial fields
  • Calculate electric field strength for point charges and uniform fields
  • Determine electric potential and electric potential energy for point charges
  • Apply Coulomb's law to calculate electrostatic forces
  • Determine the magnitude and direction of magnetic force on moving charges and current-carrying conductors
  • Analyze motion of charged particles in uniform electric and magnetic fields
  • Derive and apply orbital motion equations including orbital speed, period, and energy
  • Calculate escape velocity from gravitational fields
  • Compare and contrast gravitational, electric, and magnetic fields

Key Definitions

Field
A region of space where an object experiences a force due to its mass (gravitational), charge (electric), or motion through the field (magnetic).
Gravitational Field Strength (g)
The gravitational force per unit mass experienced by a small test mass placed at that point: g = F/m (units: N/kg or m/s²).
Electric Field Strength (E)
The electric force per unit positive charge experienced by a small test charge placed at that point: E = F/q (units: N/C or V/m).
Gravitational Potential (Vg)
The work done per unit mass in bringing a small test mass from infinity to that point in the field. Always negative for attractive fields (units: J/kg).
Electric Potential (V)
The work done per unit positive charge in bringing a small test charge from infinity to that point in the field (units: V or J/C).
Magnetic Flux Density (B)
The strength of the magnetic field, defined by the force per unit current per unit length on a conductor: B = F/(IL) (units: T or tesla).
Equipotential Surface
A surface where all points have the same potential. No work is done moving along an equipotential surface.
Escape Velocity
The minimum velocity required for an object to escape from a gravitational field to infinity without further propulsion.

Introduction to Fields

Fields are fundamental concepts in physics that describe how forces act at a distance. Rather than thinking of objects directly touching each other, we model interactions through fields that permeate space.

Prerequisites: You should be familiar with forces (Topic B), Newton's laws, energy and work (Topic B), and basic kinematics. Understanding vectors is essential.

The Field Concept: A field is a region of space where an object with certain properties (mass, charge, or motion) experiences a force. There are three fundamental fields in IB Physics:

  • Gravitational fields - Created by mass, experienced by mass
  • Electric fields - Created by charge, experienced by charge
  • Magnetic fields - Created by moving charges, experienced by moving charges

Fields can be represented using field lines, which show:

  • Direction: The direction of force on a positive test object (test mass or test charge)
  • Strength: Closer lines indicate stronger fields

Field Strength Definition: Field strength is defined as force per unit property:

  • Gravitational: \(g = F/m\) (force per unit mass)
  • Electric: \(E = F/q\) (force per unit charge)
  • Magnetic: Defined through \(F = BIL\) or \(F = qvB\)

This definition means field strength at a point is independent of the test object used to measure it.

Gravitational Fields

Newton's Law of Gravitation

The gravitational force between two point masses is given by:

$$F = \frac{Gm_1m_2}{r^2}$$

where:

  • \(G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\) N m² kg⁻² (gravitational constant)
  • \(m_1, m_2\) are the masses
  • \(r\) is the distance between centers of mass

The force is always attractive and acts along the line joining the centers.

Gravitational Field Strength

For a point mass \(M\) creating a field:

$$g = \frac{GM}{r^2}$$

This is a radial field - field strength decreases with the square of distance. Near Earth's surface, \(g \approx 9.81\) m/s² and can be treated as uniform over small height changes.

Key Insight: Gravitational field strength has the same numerical value and units as gravitational acceleration. This is because \(F = mg\) and \(F = ma\) give \(a = g\).

Gravitational Potential

Gravitational potential at a point is:

$$V_g = -\frac{GM}{r}$$

The negative sign indicates that work must be done against the field to move a mass from that point to infinity (where \(V_g = 0\) by definition).

Relationship between field strength and potential:

$$g = -\frac{dV_g}{dr}$$

In words: field strength is the negative potential gradient.

Gravitational Potential Energy

The potential energy of mass \(m\) at distance \(r\) from mass \(M\) is:

$$E_p = mV_g = -\frac{GMm}{r}$$

Note that \(E_p \to 0\) as \(r \to \infty\). This means all gravitational potential energies in bound systems are negative.

For uniform fields (near Earth's surface): \(E_p = mgh\) where \(h\) is height above reference.

Change in potential energy: \(\Delta E_p = m\Delta V_g\)

Orbital Motion

Circular Orbits

For a satellite in circular orbit, gravitational force provides centripetal force:

$$\frac{GMm}{r^2} = \frac{mv^2}{r}$$

Simplifying gives the orbital speed:

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}$$

Key observation: Orbital speed decreases with increasing orbital radius (v ∝ r⁻¹/²).

Orbital Period

Since \(v = 2\pi r/T\), combining with orbital speed:

$$T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = 2\pi r\sqrt{\frac{r}{GM}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^3}{GM}}$$

This gives Kepler's Third Law: \(T^2 \propto r^3\)

For orbits around Earth: \(T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM_E}r^3\)

Orbital Energy

Total energy of an orbiting satellite:

$$E_{total} = E_k + E_p = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 - \frac{GMm}{r}$$

Substituting \(v^2 = GM/r\):

$$E_{total} = \frac{1}{2}m\frac{GM}{r} - \frac{GMm}{r} = -\frac{GMm}{2r}$$

Critical insights:

  • Total energy is negative (satellite is bound)
  • \(E_k = \frac{GMm}{2r} = -\frac{E_{total}}{1}\) (kinetic energy is half the magnitude of potential energy)
  • To move to a higher orbit requires adding energy (making \(E_{total}\) less negative)
  • Paradoxically, satellites in higher orbits move slower but have more total energy

Escape Velocity

The minimum speed needed to escape to infinity (where \(E = 0\)):

$$\frac{1}{2}mv_e^2 - \frac{GMm}{r} = 0$$

$$v_e = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}}$$

From Earth's surface (\(r = R_E\)): \(v_e \approx 11.2\) km/s

Note: \(v_e = \sqrt{2} \times v_{orbital}\) at the same distance.

Electric Fields

Coulomb's Law

The electrostatic force between two point charges:

$$F = \frac{kq_1q_2}{r^2} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q_1q_2}{r^2}$$

where:

  • \(k = 8.99 \times 10^9\) N m² C⁻² (Coulomb's constant)
  • \(\epsilon_0 = 8.85 \times 10^{-12}\) F/m (permittivity of free space)
  • \(k = 1/(4\pi\epsilon_0)\)

Unlike gravity, electric forces can be attractive or repulsive (like charges repel, opposite charges attract).

Electric Field Strength

For a point charge \(Q\):

$$E = \frac{kQ}{r^2}$$

Direction: radially outward from positive charge, inward toward negative charge.

For uniform fields (between parallel plates):

$$E = \frac{V}{d}$$

where \(V\) is potential difference and \(d\) is separation. Field lines are parallel and equally spaced.

Electric Potential

For a point charge \(Q\):

$$V = \frac{kQ}{r}$$

Note: No negative sign (unlike gravity) because the sign of \(Q\) determines whether potential is positive or negative.

Relationship to field strength:

$$E = -\frac{dV}{dr}$$

For uniform fields: \(E = V/d\) (potential gradient is constant)

Electric Potential Energy

Potential energy of charge \(q\) at potential \(V\):

$$E_p = qV$$

For two point charges:

$$E_p = \frac{kq_1q_2}{r}$$

Sign conventions:

  • Like charges: \(E_p > 0\) (repulsive, energy stored)
  • Opposite charges: \(E_p < 0\) (attractive, bound system)

Work Done in Electric Fields

Work done moving charge \(q\) through potential difference \(\Delta V\):

$$W = q\Delta V$$

In uniform field: \(W = qEd\) where \(d\) is displacement parallel to field.

Comparing Gravitational and Electric Fields

Similarities

Both follow inverse square laws for force and field strength:

  • \(F \propto 1/r^2\)
  • Field strength \(\propto 1/r^2\)
  • Potential \(\propto 1/r\)

Both are conservative fields (work done is path-independent).

Equipotential surfaces are perpendicular to field lines for both.

Differences

Property Gravitational Electric
Always attractive/repulsive Always attractive Can be either
Property that creates field Mass Charge
Can be shielded? No Yes (conductors)
Relative strength Extremely weak Much stronger
Potential at infinity Zero Zero
Potential sign Always negative Positive or negative
Test object Test mass Test charge

Strength comparison: For a proton and electron:

  • \(F_{electric}/F_{gravitational} \approx 10^{39}\)

Electric forces dominate at atomic scales; gravity dominates at astronomical scales because matter is typically neutral.

Magnetic Fields

Magnetic Flux Density

Magnetic field strength is called magnetic flux density (\(B\)), measured in tesla (T).

Unlike gravitational and electric fields, magnetic fields do NOT follow inverse square laws (field geometry depends on source configuration).

Force on Current-Carrying Conductor

When current \(I\) flows through length \(L\) of conductor in magnetic field \(B\):

$$F = BIL\sin\theta$$

where \(\theta\) is angle between current direction and field.

Vector form: \(\vec{F} = I\vec{L} \times \vec{B}\)

Maximum force when conductor perpendicular to field (\(\theta = 90°\)): \(F = BIL\)

Zero force when conductor parallel to field (\(\theta = 0°\)).

Direction: Use the left-hand rule (or right-hand rule depending on convention):

  • First finger: Field direction
  • Second finger: Current direction
  • Thumb: Force direction

Force on Moving Charge

A charge \(q\) moving with velocity \(v\) in magnetic field \(B\):

$$F = qvB\sin\theta$$

where \(\theta\) is angle between velocity and field.

Vector form: \(\vec{F} = q\vec{v} \times \vec{B}\)

Key points:

  • Force perpendicular to both \(\vec{v}\) and \(\vec{B}\)
  • Magnetic force does NO WORK (force always perpendicular to displacement)
  • Changes direction of motion, not speed

Circular Motion in Magnetic Fields

When charged particle enters uniform magnetic field perpendicular to velocity, it undergoes circular motion.

Magnetic force provides centripetal force:

$$qvB = \frac{mv^2}{r}$$

Radius of circular path:

$$r = \frac{mv}{qB}$$

Period of rotation:

$$T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = \frac{2\pi m}{qB}$$

Note: Period is independent of speed and radius.

Applications: Mass spectrometers, cyclotrons, particle accelerators.

Motion of Charged Particles in Fields

Particle Motion in Uniform Electric Field

Consider electron or proton entering uniform electric field with initial velocity perpendicular to field.

Parallel to field direction:

  • Constant acceleration: \(a = F/m = qE/m\)
  • Equations of motion apply: \(v = u + at\), \(s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2\)

Perpendicular to field direction:

  • Constant velocity (no force component)

Result: Parabolic trajectory (analogous to projectile motion under gravity).

Energy approach: Change in kinetic energy equals work done by field:

$$\Delta E_k = qV = q E d$$

Particle Motion in Uniform Magnetic Field

If velocity parallel to field: No force, particle continues straight.

If velocity perpendicular to field: Circular motion (as derived above).

If velocity at angle to field: Helical motion (combination of circular motion perpendicular to field and constant velocity parallel to field).

Combined Electric and Magnetic Fields

Velocity Selector: Crossed electric and magnetic fields can select particles with specific velocity.

For particle moving perpendicular to both fields:

  • Electric force: \(F_E = qE\)
  • Magnetic force: \(F_B = qvB\)

For straight-line motion: \(F_E = F_B\)

$$qE = qvB$$

$$v = \frac{E}{B}$$

Only particles with this velocity pass through undeflected.

Mass Spectrometer: Combines velocity selector with magnetic field to separate ions by mass-to-charge ratio. After selection, particles enter uniform magnetic field and follow circular paths with radius:

$$r = \frac{mv}{qB}$$

Since \(v\) is known, measuring \(r\) determines \(m/q\).

Common Misconceptions and Exam Tips

Common Misconceptions

Misconception 1: Confusing field strength with force.

  • Correction: Field strength is force per unit mass/charge. It's a property of the field at a point, independent of the test object.

Misconception 2: Thinking gravitational potential energy \(mgh\) applies everywhere.

  • Correction: \(mgh\) only works for uniform fields (near Earth's surface). For radial fields, use \(E_p = -GMm/r\).

Misconception 3: Believing higher orbits require more speed.

  • Correction: Higher orbits have LOWER orbital speeds but MORE total energy.

Misconception 4: Assuming magnetic force does work.

  • Correction: Magnetic force is always perpendicular to velocity, so \(W = Fd\cos(90°) = 0\). It changes direction, not speed.

Misconception 5: Using positive values for gravitational potential.

  • Correction: Gravitational potential is always negative (or zero at infinity) because gravity is always attractive.

Misconception 6: Confusing electric potential (\(V\)) with potential energy (\(E_p\)).

  • Correction: Potential is energy per unit charge. \(E_p = qV\).

Misconception 7: Thinking equipotential surfaces are the same as field lines.

  • Correction: They're perpendicular! Field lines show force direction; equipotentials connect points of equal potential.

Practice Problems

Problem 1
A satellite of mass 500 kg orbits Earth at an altitude of 400 km above the surface. Earth's radius is 6.37 × 10⁶ m and mass is 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg. Calculate: (a) the orbital speed of the satellite, (b) the orbital period in minutes, and (c) the total energy of the satellite.

Solution

(a) Orbital radius: \(r = R_E + h = 6.37 \times 10^6 + 4.00 \times 10^5 = 6.77 \times 10^6\) m

Orbital speed: \(v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}} = \sqrt{\frac{(6.67 \times 10^{-11})(5.97 \times 10^{24})}{6.77 \times 10^6}}\)

\(v = \sqrt{5.88 \times 10^7} = 7.67 \times 10^3\) m/s = 7.67 km/s

(b) Period: \(T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = \frac{2\pi (6.77 \times 10^6)}{7.67 \times 10^3} = 5.55 \times 10^3\) s

Converting: \(T = 5550/60 = 92.5\) minutes

(c) Total energy: \(E = -\frac{GMm}{2r} = -\frac{(6.67 \times 10^{-11})(5.97 \times 10^{24})(500)}{2(6.77 \times 10^6)}\)

\(E = -1.47 \times 10^{10}\) J = -14.7 GJ

Explanation: This problem tests orbital mechanics fundamentals. Note that we use the total orbital radius (Earth radius + altitude), not just the altitude. The negative total energy indicates the satellite is in a bound orbit. The period of about 92 minutes is typical for low Earth orbit.

Problem 2
Two point charges, \(q_1 = +3.0\) μC and \(q_2 = -5.0\) μC, are separated by 40 cm. Calculate: (a) the electric force between them, (b) the electric field strength at the midpoint between them, and (c) the electric potential at the midpoint.

Solution

(a) Using Coulomb's law:

\(F = \frac{kq_1q_2}{r^2} = \frac{(8.99 \times 10^9)(3.0 \times 10^{-6})(5.0 \times 10^{-6})}{(0.40)^2}\)

\(F = 0.843\) N (attractive, so toward each other)

(b) At midpoint (\(r = 0.20\) m from each charge):

Field from \(q_1\) (pointing away): \(E_1 = \frac{kq_1}{r^2} = \frac{(8.99 \times 10^9)(3.0 \times 10^{-6})}{(0.20)^2} = 6.74 \times 10^5\) N/C (to the right)

Field from \(q_2\) (pointing toward): \(E_2 = \frac{k|q_2|}{r^2} = \frac{(8.99 \times 10^9)(5.0 \times 10^{-6})}{(0.20)^2} = 1.12 \times 10^6\) N/C (to the right)

Both point in same direction (toward negative charge), so:

\(E_{total} = E_1 + E_2 = 1.79 \times 10^6\) N/C toward \(q_2\)

(c) Potential at midpoint:

\(V = V_1 + V_2 = \frac{kq_1}{r} + \frac{kq_2}{r} = \frac{k(q_1 + q_2)}{r}\)

\(V = \frac{(8.99 \times 10^9)(3.0 - 5.0) \times 10^{-6}}{0.20} = -8.99 \times 10^4\) V = -89.9 kV

Explanation: For part (b), both charges create fields pointing toward the negative charge at the midpoint, so they add. For part (c), potential is a scalar, so we add algebraically considering signs. The negative potential indicates work would be needed to bring a positive test charge from infinity to this point.

Problem 3
An electron enters a uniform magnetic field of 0.50 T with velocity 2.0 × 10⁷ m/s perpendicular to the field. Calculate the radius of the circular path and the period of rotation. (Electron: mass = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg, charge = -1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C)

Solution

Radius of circular path:

\(r = \frac{mv}{|q|B} = \frac{(9.11 \times 10^{-31})(2.0 \times 10^7)}{(1.60 \times 10^{-19})(0.50)}\)

\(r = \frac{1.822 \times 10^{-23}}{8.0 \times 10^{-20}} = 2.28 \times 10^{-4}\) m = 0.228 mm

Period:

\(T = \frac{2\pi m}{|q|B} = \frac{2\pi (9.11 \times 10^{-31})}{(1.60 \times 10^{-19})(0.50)}\)

\(T = \frac{5.72 \times 10^{-30}}{8.0 \times 10^{-20}} = 7.15 \times 10^{-11}\) s = 71.5 ps

Alternatively: \(T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = \frac{2\pi (2.28 \times 10^{-4})}{2.0 \times 10^7} = 7.16 \times 10^{-11}\) s (confirms answer)

Explanation: Note we use the magnitude of the electron's charge. The small radius (0.2 mm) and short period (picoseconds) are typical for electron motion in magnetic fields. This principle is used in cathode ray tubes and particle accelerators.

Problem 4
Calculate the escape velocity from: (a) Earth's surface (mass = 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg, radius = 6.37 × 10⁶ m), and (b) the Moon's surface (mass = 7.35 × 10²² kg, radius = 1.74 × 10⁶ m). Comment on the significance of these values.

Solution

(a) Earth's escape velocity:

\(v_e = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(6.67 \times 10^{-11})(5.97 \times 10^{24})}{6.37 \times 10^6}}\)

\(v_e = \sqrt{1.25 \times 10^8} = 1.12 \times 10^4\) m/s = 11.2 km/s

(b) Moon's escape velocity:

\(v_e = \sqrt{\frac{2(6.67 \times 10^{-11})(7.35 \times 10^{22})}{1.74 \times 10^6}}\)

\(v_e = \sqrt{5.63 \times 10^6} = 2.37 \times 10^3\) m/s = 2.37 km/s

Comment: The Moon's escape velocity is about 5 times smaller than Earth's, making it much easier to launch spacecraft from the Moon. This also explains why the Moon has no atmosphere - gas molecules can reach escape velocity at typical temperatures and escape into space.

Explanation: Escape velocity depends on both mass and radius of the body. Despite having 81 times less mass than Earth, the Moon's escape velocity is only about 5 times smaller because its radius is also smaller. The relationship \(v_e \propto \sqrt{M/R}\) shows both factors matter.

Problem 5
A uniform electric field of strength 5.0 × 10³ N/C exists between two parallel plates separated by 8.0 cm. An electron is released from rest at the negative plate. Calculate: (a) the acceleration of the electron, (b) its velocity when it reaches the positive plate, and (c) the time taken.

Solution

(a) Force on electron: \(F = eE = (1.60 \times 10^{-19})(5.0 \times 10^3) = 8.0 \times 10^{-16}\) N

Acceleration: \(a = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{8.0 \times 10^{-16}}{9.11 \times 10^{-31}} = 8.78 \times 10^{14}\) m/s²

(b) Using energy method (preferred):

Work done: \(W = eEd = (1.60 \times 10^{-19})(5.0 \times 10^3)(0.080) = 6.4 \times 10^{-17}\) J

This equals kinetic energy gained:

\(\frac{1}{2}mv^2 = 6.4 \times 10^{-17}\)

\(v = \sqrt{\frac{2(6.4 \times 10^{-17})}{9.11 \times 10^{-31}}} = 1.19 \times 10^7\) m/s

Alternatively using kinematics: \(v^2 = u^2 + 2as = 0 + 2(8.78 \times 10^{14})(0.080) = 1.40 \times 10^{14}\)

\(v = 1.18 \times 10^7\) m/s (agrees within rounding)

(c) Time: \(v = u + at\)

\(t = \frac{v}{a} = \frac{1.19 \times 10^7}{8.78 \times 10^{14}} = 1.35 \times 10^{-8}\) s = 13.5 ns

Explanation: The energy method is often simpler and avoids accumulated rounding errors. The final speed (about 4% of light speed) is high enough that we're approaching the regime where relativistic effects would become noticeable, though we ignore them at IB level. Note the extremely short time - electrons respond very quickly to electric fields.

Exam Tips

Always draw diagrams for field problems - show field lines, forces, and directions clearly
For orbital problems, remember the counterintuitive result: higher orbit = slower speed but more energy
Check signs carefully: gravitational potential is always negative; electric potential depends on source charge sign
Use energy methods when possible - often simpler than force/kinematics approaches
Remember magnetic force does no work (always perpendicular to motion) - it changes direction, not speed
For particle motion in fields, consider components parallel and perpendicular to field separately
Know the key relationships: orbital speed v ∝ r⁻¹/², period T² ∝ r³, energy E ∝ -1/r
Unit conversions are common sources of errors - write units throughout calculations
For comparison questions between gravitational and electric fields, organize by mathematical similarities first, then physical differences
Remember equipotential surfaces are perpendicular to field lines - no work is done moving along an equipotential
For data-based questions, expect to linearize relationships (e.g., plot T² vs r³ to verify Kepler's law)
When dealing with satellites, always use total orbital radius (planet radius + altitude), not just altitude
