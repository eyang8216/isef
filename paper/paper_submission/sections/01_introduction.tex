\section{Introduction}

A Taylor cone is the conical liquid interface that can form when electric Maxwell stress competes with capillary stress. In the ideal charge-free, perfectly conducting limit, its classical half-angle is $\alpha_{\mathrm{T}}\approx\SI{49.29}{\degree}$ \cite{taylor1964disintegration}. \rev{The fluid dynamics of Taylor cones connect this static equilibrium to moving interfaces, charge transport, and cone-jet emission \cite{fernandez2007fluid}.}

\rev{Interfacial stress-balance theory \cite{melcher1969electrohydrodynamics} and the Taylor--Melcher leaky-dielectric model \cite{saville1997electrohydrodynamics} describe how electrical stresses interact with fluid motion and surface charge. Studies of emitted current \cite{fernandez1994current} and cone-jet scaling \cite{ganan1997conejet} relate emission behavior to liquid properties and flow rate, while coupled cone-and-jet models calculate interface shape, electric fields, and surface charge \cite{hartman1999electrohydrodynamic}. Experiments on cone-jet operation \cite{cloupeau1989conejet} and classifications of spraying modes \cite{cloupeau1994modes} further show the importance of specifying the operating regime when interpreting observed shapes.}

\rev{Against this background, the present study examines the geometric information carried by two observables in a static model. Reliable angle identification requires a diagnostic that responds sufficiently to changes in candidate geometry. Comparing its sensitivity under controlled numerical and boundary settings provides a way to assess that requirement.}

This paper addresses \rev{the following} numerical question: once an electric field has been computed around a candidate cone, does the selected observable identify its half-angle? We compare the minimum of an amplitude-projected stress residual with the crossing of a fitted flank-field power-law exponent. The distinction matters because a well-resolved field does not by itself make an inverse geometric estimate unique: a shallow objective can assign nearly equal values to distinct geometries. \rev{The question therefore applies independently of solver accuracy: a high-fidelity computation can still deliver an unreliable angle if the observable is insensitive, and much of the established cone-angle literature rests on observables whose discriminating power has not been systematically compared.}

The solver is a two-dimensional axisymmetric finite-difference workflow with an immersed representation of the liquid interface (Figure~\ref{fig:system-schematic}). Manufactured solutions and curved-boundary refinement test components of the electrostatic calculation. Parameter and initialization studies then compare the two angle diagnostics. A separate image and stress-analysis workflow is demonstrated on published ethanol cone-jet figures\rev{, extending the analysis to secondary-data geometry extraction and fixed-pressure stress diagnosis}.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item We show that, for the tested restricted cone family, amplitude-projected stress residuals are weakly angle-discriminating: their minima move by several degrees with numerical choices and initialization while their values change little.
\item We show that, with the analytical Taylor potential imposed at the far boundary, the $p=-1$ flank-field crossing is substantially less variable across the tested configurations ($49.07^\circ$--$49.25^\circ$). This is a consistency check of the imposed Taylor far field, not an independent physical-angle prediction. With a grounded outer boundary, no $p=-1$ crossing occurs.
\item \rev{We place the two diagnostics in context. The crossing diagnostic is less established than residual minimization and is assessed here beside it; both are implemented in an inspectable, open, consumer-hardware solver, which yields a transparent angle-assessment tool and a basis for traceable checks in electrospinning and electrospray applications. The implications remain provisional because the evidence is numerical and the model is reduced-order.}
\item We document the numerical and image-processing evidence with its provenance: reproducible manufactured, refinement, multistart, and synthetic tests; a retained FEM comparison whose source and mesh are absent; and a published-image workflow whose extracted intermediates are absent.
\end{enumerate}
